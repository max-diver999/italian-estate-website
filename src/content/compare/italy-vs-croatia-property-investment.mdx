**Quick answer:** The comparison everyone makes, 9% in Italy against 3% in Croatia, compares two different bases. Croatia's 3% is charged on market value. Italy's second-home rate, on a resale from a private seller to an individual who asks for it, is charged on cadastral value, which is normally far below the price paid.

Cross-border property comparisons are usually settled by a table of headline rates, and the table is usually wrong. The Italian and Croatian purchase taxes are the clearest example of it in the Adriatic, because the two countries do not tax the same number.

## The rule

**Compare the tax payable, not the rate.** Work out the actual Italian charge for the specific property using its cadastral value, and the actual Croatian charge using the market value the tax authority would apply. The difference between those two numbers is the real difference, and it is usually much smaller than the headline suggests.

**The condition that reverses it:** on a new build the comparison changes completely, because both countries move to VAT and the transfer-tax point disappears. A buyer choosing between two off-plan apartments is running a different calculation from one choosing between two resales.

## What Italy actually charges, and on what

Italian registration tax on a second-home resale is levied at a rate that looks high next to Croatia's. The base is what matters.

Under the prezzo-valore mechanism, on a residential purchase by an individual from a private seller, the tax is computed on the property's cadastral value rather than on the price agreed. The buyer has to request it in the deed; it is not applied automatically.

| Element | Italian position |
| --- | --- |
| Nominal second-home rate | High relative to Croatia |
| Base on a private resale, if requested | Cadastral value |
| Base if not requested, or if the seller is a business | The price, or VAT rules apply |
| Effect | The effective rate on the price paid is far below the nominal rate |
| Requirement | The election must be made in the deed |

Italian cadastral values were set on a historic basis and sit well below market prices, with the gap varying by location and by category. That is why the effective Italian charge on an ordinary resale is frequently a fraction of what a naive calculation on the price produces.

The [registration tax guide](/guides/italy-registration-tax-property/) sets out the rates and the mechanism properly, and the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/) puts it alongside the notary, agency and search costs that also fall due.

## What Croatia charges, and on what

Real estate transfer tax at 3%, computed on market value rather than on the price stated in the purchase agreement. That last point is important in both directions: it means the rate is honest, and it means understating the price in the contract achieves nothing, because the authority values the property itself.

Where the seller is a developer registered for VAT, the transaction is subject to VAT at the standard rate instead and the 3% does not apply. Buyers of new-build apartments are therefore in the VAT system, not the transfer-tax system.

Croatia has also introduced relief for first-time buyers, applying to contracts or occupancy from 1 January 2025, giving a full refund of transfer tax and up to half of the VAT, subject to limits by size and by local prices. That is a meaningful benefit for the buyer it fits and irrelevant to most foreign purchasers of second homes.

## Running the numbers properly

The method is short and almost nobody uses it.

1. Obtain the cadastral value of the specific Italian property, from the visura, rather than estimating it
2. Compute the Italian registration tax on that value at the applicable rate
3. Add the fixed cadastral and mortgage duties, the notary and the agency commission
4. For the Croatian alternative, apply the transfer tax to market value, not to the asking price
5. Compare the two totals in currency, on comparable properties, rather than comparing percentages

Anyone who does this discovers that the purchase-tax difference is real but modest, and that it is dwarfed by the price difference between the two markets and by the transaction costs that are not taxes at all.

## What actually differs between the two markets

Once the tax point is settled honestly, four genuine differences remain, and they should drive the decision.

**Market depth.** Italy has one of the largest property markets in Europe with a long-established international buyer base. Croatia's is much smaller and much younger, with thinner resale outside the best-known locations.

**Price level.** Croatian coastal property is cheaper than the comparable Italian coast, which is the actual attraction and does not need a tax argument to support it.

**Regulation of letting.** Both countries regulate short letting and both have been tightening. The rules differ by municipality in Italy and by local and national provision in Croatia, and neither should be assumed from the other.

**Distance and access.** For most northern European buyers the two are comparable by air and very different by road, which matters for a property visited several times a year.

## The costs neither country's headline mentions

Both comparisons above are about taxes. In practice the taxes are rarely the largest line in either country.

In Italy, the notary is a public officer whose fee is a real cost, the agency commission is customarily paid by both sides, and searches and translations add up. The [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/) breaks the total down, and the sum of the non-tax items is frequently comparable to the registration tax itself.

In Croatia, the equivalent items are the lawyer, the notary, the agency and the land registry, and the market convention on who pays what differs from the Italian one. A buyer should ask for a written estimate of total costs in each country rather than assuming the tax rate is the comparison.

Currency is the other invisible item. Croatia adopted the euro, which removes the exchange question that used to complicate the comparison for eurozone buyers and leaves it for everyone else on the same footing as Italy.

## What a buyer should actually do

1. Pick two comparable properties, one in each country, at realistic prices
2. Obtain the Italian cadastral value from the visura, not from an estimate
3. Ask a Croatian lawyer what the tax authority would value the Croatian property at
4. Get a full written cost estimate for each transaction, taxes and fees together
5. Compare the totals, then ask which market you could sell in more easily

Step five decides more outcomes than steps one to four combined. A difference of a few thousand euro in purchase costs is recovered or lost in a single negotiation; a market where the property takes two years to sell is a different asset class.

## Where the Adriatic comparison actually bites

For a buyer specifically weighing the two sides of the same sea, the useful contrast is between the Croatian coast and the Italian Adriatic rather than between Croatia and Tuscany. [Termoli](/areas/termoli/) and [Pescara](/areas/pescara/) are the Italian markets that compete most directly on price and on sea, and both have year-round economies that a Croatian resort island does not.

Buyers comparing Italy against other Mediterranean options on residence or tax grounds should read the [Greece comparison](/compare/italy-vs-greece-property-investment/) and the [Malta comparison](/compare/italy-vs-malta-property-investment/), both of which turn on programmes rather than on transfer taxes.

## What the Italian base actually looks like

Because prezzo-valore is the hinge of this whole comparison, it is worth being concrete about what a cadastral value is and why it is low.

Italian cadastral values derive from a rental income estimate assigned to each property by category, class and size, updated by statutory multipliers rather than by market movement. The result is a value that bears a historic relationship to the market rather than a current one, and the gap varies: it tends to be widest in the locations where market prices have risen most, which is precisely where foreign buyers concentrate.

Two consequences follow for a buyer running this comparison.

The saving is largest where the price is highest relative to the cadastral value, which means the Italian advantage on purchase tax is greatest in the desirable locations rather than in the cheap ones.

The value is knowable in advance. It is on the visura for the specific property and a buyer can obtain it before making an offer, which turns this from a general argument into an actual number for the actual property.

- Ask for the visura before the offer, not at the deed
- Compute the registration tax on the cadastral value at the applicable rate
- Confirm with the notary that the election will be made in the deed
- Remember it does not apply where the seller is a business charging VAT

## A note on what the two markets are for

Behind the tax arithmetic sit two different propositions, and it helps to say which is which.

Italy is a mature market with a very large stock, an established international buyer base, high prices in the sought-after locations and correspondingly deep resale. A buyer there is competing with a lot of other buyers and can expect to be able to sell.

Croatia is a younger market with a shorter modern history of foreign ownership, lower prices, and resale that depends heavily on location. A buyer there is paying less and accepting a narrower exit.

Those are the real terms of the trade, and neither transfer tax changes them.

## Letting rules, which move faster than tax rules

A buyer intending to let should note that both countries have been tightening short-let regulation and that the tightening is local as well as national.

In Italy the national framework requires a registration code and imposes reporting obligations, and on top of that individual comuni have been restricting new tourist letting in their historic centres. That municipal layer is where the real constraint sits, and it varies from one town to the next.

Croatia regulates tourist accommodation through its own licensing system with local application, and its coastal municipalities have their own pressures and their own responses.

The practical point for this comparison is that neither country's letting position can be read off a national rule. A buyer should check the specific municipality in either country before assuming that a letting business is available at the address, and should assume that the direction of travel in both is toward more restriction rather than less. That check costs an email to a local agent or lawyer and it is the difference between buying an asset and buying a business that turns out not to be licensable.

## Two things to settle before signing anywhere

The Italian election has to be requested. Prezzo-valore is not automatic, and a buyer who does not ask, or whose notary is not instructed, pays on the price. It is worth confirming in writing before the deed is drafted.

The Croatian base is the authority's valuation. A purchase agreement showing a low price does not reduce the charge, and a buyer told otherwise is being told something that is not true.

The [prima casa comparison](/guides/italy-prima-casa-vs-second-home-tax/) explains the Italian reduced-rate route and its residence condition, and the [selling guide](/guides/selling-property-italy-foreigner/) sets out what happens on the way out, which is where a thin resale market costs more than a purchase tax ever did.
