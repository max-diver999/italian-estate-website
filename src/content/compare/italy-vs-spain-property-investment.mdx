**Quick answer:** Above a threshold, Spain charges you every year simply for owning the asset. Italy does not. That single structural difference outweighs most of the yield and price comparisons that dominate discussion of the two markets, and it becomes decisive at exactly the value level most international buyers are working at.

<TldrBlock text="Spanish wealth tax: state exemption of €700,000 of net wealth per person, applying to non-residents on Spanish assets · a further allowance of up to €300,000 against a main residence, for residents only · state scale progressive from 0.2% to 3.5% · autonomous communities modify the position, and a solidarity charge reaches higher wealth even where they rebate it · Italy has no equivalent charge, only IMU on cadastral value" />

Buyers comparing the two countries usually compare climate, price per square metre and rental yield. Those are real variables and they are not the one that decides the outcome for a buyer above a certain value. The decisive difference is that one of the two countries taxes the holding itself.

## The rule

**If your net Spanish assets, after deducting acquisition debt, would exceed the exempt threshold, Spain imposes an annual charge that Italy does not.** That charge scales with value, recurs every year of ownership, and is unrelated to whether the property produces any income.

**Below that level, the comparison reverts to the ordinary variables.** Purchase costs, yields, management, flight connections and preference, none of which favours either country decisively.

**The condition that reverses it:** an autonomous community that fully rebates the wealth tax changes the arithmetic for property sitting in it, which is why the region within Spain matters as much as the value does. The solidarity charge aimed at higher net wealth was designed to reach through exactly that rebate, so the escape is partial and depends on the value level.

## What Spain actually charges

The Spanish wealth tax applies to residents on worldwide assets and to non-residents on assets situated in Spain, which for most foreign owners means the property itself.

| Element | Position |
| --- | --- |
| State exemption | €700,000 of net wealth per person |
| Main residence allowance | Up to €300,000, residents only |
| Rate scale | Progressive, 0.2% to 3.5% |
| Base | Net wealth, so secured debt reduces it |
| Regional variation | Autonomous communities modify the outcome |
| Higher-wealth charge | A separate solidarity tax reaches above the ordinary thresholds |

Three features deserve emphasis for a foreign buyer.

The exemption is per person. A couple buying jointly has two exemptions between them, which moves the threshold at which the charge starts to bite and makes ownership structure a live question rather than an afterthought.

The base is net. Outstanding debt secured on the Spanish property reduces the taxable amount, which means a mortgage has a tax effect in Spain that it does not have in Italy. Buyers who would otherwise pay cash should model both.

The main residence allowance does not help a holiday-home buyer, because it is available to residents. The typical northern European purchaser of a Spanish coastal apartment is a non-resident, and gets the €700,000 and nothing further.

## What Italy charges instead

Italy has no wealth tax on property held by a non-resident. What it has is IMU, a municipal property tax calculated on cadastral value with rates set locally within national bands, and the [IMU guide](/guides/imu-property-tax-italy/) sets out how it is computed and what a second home pays.

The distinction matters and it is not merely terminological.

- IMU is calculated on cadastral value, which for most Italian property sits well below market value
- It does not scale into higher brackets as the owner's total wealth rises
- It is unaffected by how many other properties the owner holds outside Italy
- There is no threshold above which a second, wealth-based charge appears
- The rate is a municipal decision within national limits, so it is knowable in advance for a specific address

Italy's costs are front-loaded instead. Purchase taxes are meaningful and are covered in the [registration tax guide](/guides/italy-registration-tax-property/) and the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/). A buyer pays more at the start in Italy and less each year afterwards.

## Working the comparison honestly

The temptation is to convert this into a verdict. It does not produce one, because the answer depends on value, on holding period and on region.

**A modest holiday apartment.** Below the Spanish threshold, the wealth tax is irrelevant and the comparison is about yield, costs and preference. Spain's transaction costs and its rental markets in some regions are genuinely competitive.

**A substantial property.** Above the threshold, Spain adds an annual charge computed on value and Italy does not. Over a holding period measured in decades, that compounds into a large number, and it should be modelled rather than noted.

**A portfolio.** For a buyer accumulating assets in one country, the Spanish charge scales with the total while Italy's IMU scales only with the number of properties and their cadastral values. The gap widens as the portfolio grows.

**A relocation.** For someone becoming resident, the comparison changes shape entirely, because Spanish residents are charged on worldwide net wealth. That is a very different question from a non-resident holding one property, and it needs advice rather than an article.

## The ownership structure question

Because the Spanish exemption is per person, how a property is held changes the outcome, and it is one of the few decisions a buyer controls entirely.

A couple buying jointly has two exemptions. A single owner holding the same property has one. A property held through a company is a different analysis again and brings its own consequences, some of them unfavourable, which is why it should never be adopted on the strength of one tax point.

Italy's position is unaffected by any of this. IMU attaches to the property and its cadastral value, apportioned between owners, and no threshold appears at any level of total wealth. A buyer structuring an Italian purchase is doing so for succession, for privacy or for management reasons rather than to reduce an annual charge that does not exist.

That asymmetry is worth naming because it changes what professional advice is for in each country. In Spain, a structuring conversation before purchase can materially change the annual cost. In Italy, it cannot, and a buyer who is being sold an elaborate Italian holding structure on tax grounds should ask precisely which tax it reduces.

## What each country does on the way out

Purchase and holding are two of three stages, and the exit is where several comparisons quietly reverse.

Italy exempts a private seller's gain on residential property after five years of ownership, which for a long-term holder removes Italian capital gains tax on the sale entirely. That is unusually generous by European standards and it is a large part of why Italian holding periods are long.

Spain taxes non-residents on gains at a flat rate, with a withholding of 3% of the price taken from the sale proceeds by the buyer and paid over on account. There is no equivalent of the Italian five-year exemption for a non-resident holiday-home owner.

- A long-term Italian holder can exit without Italian tax on the gain
- A Spanish non-resident seller faces a charge on the gain and a withholding mechanism at completion
- That difference compounds with the annual wealth charge rather than offsetting it
- Any comparison that stops at the purchase stage has looked at the smallest of the three

## What this page is not saying

It is not saying that Italy is cheaper. Italian purchase taxes on a second home are substantial, Italian bureaucracy is slower than most buyers expect, and Italian rental yields in the markets foreign buyers prefer are modest. The [selling guide](/guides/selling-property-italy-foreigner/) sets out the exit, and the [cedolare secca guide](/guides/cedolare-secca-italy-rental-property/) the income position.

It is saying that one specific and often-overlooked difference is structural rather than marginal, and that a buyer who models purchase costs and yields without modelling the annual holding charge has compared the wrong things.

For the other European comparisons on the same axis, [Portugal](/compare/italy-vs-portugal-property-investment/) and [Greece](/compare/italy-vs-greece-property-investment/) each turn on residence programmes rather than on holding taxes, while [France](/compare/italy-vs-france-property-investment/) is the closest analogue to Spain because it also taxes real estate wealth above a threshold.

## Why this difference gets missed

Three reasons, and knowing them is most of the defence.

Purchase costs are quoted and holding costs are not. Every agent in both countries will produce a purchase cost estimate. Almost none will produce a ten-year holding cost projection, because it is not their job and because it does not help a sale.

The threshold sits above where most published comparisons stop. Articles comparing the two countries tend to model a typical apartment, which falls below the Spanish exemption, and conclude correctly that the wealth tax does not apply. The buyer at twice that value reads the same article.

Regional variation makes the Spanish position hard to state simply. Because autonomous communities modify the outcome, no single number is correct for the whole country, which encourages writers to omit the subject rather than qualify it. The solidarity charge complicates it further by reaching over regional rebates at higher wealth levels.

The consequence is that a buyer can read a great deal about both markets without encountering the one structural difference that will cost them most.

## A worked comparison of shape

Consider two purchases of equivalent value, one on each coast, held for a long period by a non-resident couple.

In Italy the costs are front-loaded: registration tax and fees at purchase, then IMU annually on cadastral value, then no Italian tax on the gain if the property is held beyond the five-year point and sold privately.

In Spain the costs are spread and back-loaded: transfer tax and fees at purchase, then the annual wealth charge on the amount above the exemptions, then tax on the gain at disposal with a withholding taken at completion.

The Italian profile suits a long hold by someone who wants predictability. The Spanish profile suits a shorter hold below the exemption threshold. Neither is universally better, and the mistake is to compare only the first line of each.

## What changes if you become resident

Everything, and buyers who are contemplating a move rather than a second home should treat this page as background rather than as an answer.

A Spanish tax resident is charged on worldwide net wealth rather than only on Spanish assets, which turns a charge on one property into a charge on a whole balance sheet. An Italian tax resident faces Italian taxes on worldwide income and a separate small charge on foreign financial assets and foreign property, but no general wealth tax.

Italy also operates optional regimes for new residents that have no Spanish equivalent in the same form, including a substitute tax on foreign income for high-net-worth arrivals and a reduced rate for foreign pensioners moving to qualifying southern comuni. Those regimes change the comparison entirely for the households they fit.

The instruction is simple: if residence is on the table, stop comparing property taxes and get advice on both systems as a whole, because the property is the small part of that decision. Italy's regime for new residents runs for up to 15 years and its regime for foreign pensioners for 10 years, and both are elections made at the point of arrival rather than adjustments available later, so the sequencing of a move matters as much as its destination.

## Practical steps before deciding

1. Establish the likely net Spanish position after debt, per owner, not per couple
2. Identify the autonomous community and obtain its current treatment, since it may rebate or not
3. Check whether the solidarity charge would reach the value level in question
4. Model the Italian alternative on purchase cost plus annual IMU, using a real cadastral value rather than an estimate
5. Compare over the intended holding period, not over one year

Both countries change these rules. The figures here reflect the position at the time of writing and the Spanish part varies by region as well as by year, so a Spanish adviser should confirm the current treatment for the specific property before anyone signs anything.
