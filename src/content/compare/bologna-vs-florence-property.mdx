**Quick answer:** Both cities have a housing shortage and have chosen opposite remedies. Bologna is adding beds, including thousands of purpose-built student rooms that compete with private landlords. Florence is restricting the tourist use of existing stock, which removes a business model without adding a single room.

<TldrBlock text="Bologna: public residences around 1,900 beds, private operators past 2,200 and building, roughly 3,100 beds available with about 2,300 more planned · one residence opening September 2026 with 633 beds · the national programme targets 40,000 to 100,000 beds by June 2026 with 1.2 billion euro, and has disbursed under a fifth of it · Florence: short lets in the core capped at units operating during 2024, no new authorisations in the UNESCO area" />

Two cities 100 kilometres apart, both with more people wanting housing than housing available, and two policy responses that point in opposite directions. For a buyer the question is not which city is nicer. It is which policy risk sits on the property they are about to buy.

## Which policy risk do you want

**Buy in Florence if you want policy that removes competing uses.** Restricting tourist letting pushes stock back toward residential occupation and supports residential demand from a landlord's point of view.

**Buy in Bologna if you want an economy with four legs and can tolerate new supply arriving in your segment.** The city is adding student beds at scale, and a landlord letting rooms at the upper end is competing with them.

**The condition that reverses it:** delivery. Bologna's competing supply is a threat in proportion to how much of it is actually built and occupied, and the national programme funding it has run well behind its own schedule. Slippage protects the incumbent landlord; acceleration does not.

## What Bologna is building

The numbers are large relative to the size of the market.

| Component | Position |
| --- | --- |
| Public residences | Around 1,900 beds |
| Private operators | Already past 2,200 beds and still building |
| City total reported | Roughly 3,100 beds available |
| Planned additions | About 2,300 more |
| One project alone | 633 beds, opening September 2026 |

The funding comes largely from the national recovery programme, which set out to raise student accommodation from 40,000 beds to 100,000 by June 2026, with 1.2 billion euro allocated. Disbursement has run well behind that target nationally, which is a fact a landlord should hold onto: an announced bed is not a competing bed until it opens.

Two features of Bologna's delivery matter for a private landlord.

The beneficiaries have been mostly private operators rather than public bodies, which means the new supply is commercial and priced commercially. Reports from the city indicate that private residences frequently charge above local market rents.

The public component is small by comparison. The regional right-to-study agency's own stock in the city is measured in tens of beds from recent additions rather than in hundreds, which tells you where the volume is actually coming from.

## Who that competition actually reaches

Not every private landlord in Bologna is exposed to the same degree, and the distinction is worth drawing carefully.

- **Premium rooms let to international students** compete directly with purpose-built residences, which offer services, security and a contract in English that a private landlord struggles to match
- **Plain flats let to groups of domestic students** compete much less, because the price point is below what commercial residences target
- **Flats let to non-students**, to hospital staff, to professionals or to families, are barely affected at all
- **Short lets to visitors** are a different market again, subject to national and municipal licensing rather than to student supply

A landlord who intends to serve the first category should read the pipeline carefully. One serving the third is largely insulated, and Bologna's hospital system and professional economy make that a real option in a way it is not in a smaller university town.

## What Florence did instead

Restricted a use. In the historic core, tourist letting was frozen at the units already regularly operating during 2024; no new authorisations are issued inside the UNESCO area; key boxes on facades are banned and let apartments must meet a minimum size, with the freeze later extended beyond the UNESCO boundary. The [Florence page](/areas/florence/) sets out the mechanics.

The effect on supply is the opposite of Bologna's. No new dwellings are created; existing dwellings are pushed out of tourist use and back toward residential letting or owner occupation. For a residential landlord that is straightforwardly favourable: the competition for tenants falls and the stock available for letting rises.

For a landlord whose business was tourist letting it is the reverse, and for a buyer arriving now it means the tourist route is closed on new entrants in the core.

## Comparing the two as places to own property

Set the policy aside and the cities differ on the fundamentals.

**Bologna** has a large and ancient university, one of Italy's major hospital systems, manufacturing in the surrounding region, an exhibition and trade fair business, and the country's central high-speed rail interchange. That is four or five independent demand sources. Its distinctive property question is the portico: a privately owned covered pavement whose maintenance falls on the building's owners, set out on the [Bologna page](/areas/bologna/).

**Florence** has tourism, culture, artisanal production, universities and international study programmes, and a smaller professional base. Its distinctive property question is now regulatory. The [Bologna guide](/guides/bologna-property-investment-guide/) and the [Florence guide](/guides/florence-property-investment-guide/) cover the two markets in detail.

On price, Florence's prime historic stock is materially more expensive than Bologna's equivalent, and Bologna's yields on residential letting are correspondingly better. Comparing them properly means separate void and management assumptions for a student let and a professional one, built the way the [rental yield guide](/guides/italy-rental-yield-guide/) describes.

## What a landlord should actually watch in each

In Bologna, the pipeline. Which projects are funded, which are under construction, which have opened, and at what price point. That information is public and it is the single most useful input into a Bologna letting decision.

In Florence, the municipal register and the boundary. Which zones are restricted, whether the block has extended again, and whether a specific unit holds an established position. That is also public and it changes by council decision rather than by construction.

Both are checkable before purchase. Neither appears in a listing.

For a third option in the same region with a different tenant class entirely, [Parma](/areas/parma/) has a demand source neither of these cities has. For the Tuscan alternative to Florence, the [Florence and Siena comparison](/compare/florence-vs-siena-property/) sets out what happens when a city loses its main employer, and the [Milan and Florence comparison](/compare/milan-vs-florence-property-investment/) covers the choice for a buyer whose objective is Italian residence rather than a rental yield.

## Reading the Bologna pipeline

The information a landlord needs is public and it repays the hour it takes to assemble.

Identify the funded projects, their bed counts and their expected opening dates. Establish which are under construction and which are still at planning, because the gap between those two states has been where most of the national programme's slippage has occurred.

Establish the price point each operator is targeting. Purpose-built accommodation charging above local market rents competes for a specific student and leaves the rest of the market alone.

Map the locations against the faculties they serve. A residence on one side of the city does not compete with private stock on the other in the way a citywide bed count suggests.

Then reassess annually, because the pipeline moves.

## The portico and the register, in passing

Each city has a property question that has nothing to do with housing policy and that a buyer will meet regardless.

In Bologna it is the portico: the covered pavement in front of a building is private property under public use, and its maintenance falls on the owners above it. That is an obligation attached to a large share of the central stock and it arrives as extraordinary works.

In Florence it is the register: whether a specific unit holds an established short-let position, which is now a valuable and verifiable attribute rather than a formality.

Neither is a reason to prefer one city. Both are reasons to instruct someone locally rather than to buy on a portal listing.

## What each city costs to enter

Florence's prime historic stock is materially more expensive per square metre than Bologna's equivalent, and the gap widens in the centre.

That has a direct consequence for yields. The same rent against a lower purchase price produces a better return, and Bologna's residential letting yields are consistently better than Florence's for comparable quality. What Florence offers instead is scarcity, international recognition and a resale market that reaches beyond Italy.

A buyer choosing on numbers alone should look at Bologna. A buyer choosing on the character of the asset will keep looking at Florence, and should do so with the yield understood rather than assumed.

## The practical research each city needs

For Bologna: the student housing pipeline, project by project, with opening dates and price points; and the condominium position on any porticoed building, since the covered pavement is a private liability.

For Florence: the current municipal position on short letting for the specific address, and whether a unit being sold as a going concern actually holds an established position that survives a transfer.

Both are checkable before an offer. Neither is on a listing, and in both cities the buyers who lose money are the ones who did not ask.

## A summary a buyer can act on

Bologna: a diversified economy, better yields, cheaper entry, and new supply arriving in the student segment. Watch the pipeline, buy stock that serves more than students, and read the condominium position on any porticoed building.

Florence: a narrower economy, higher prices, thinner yields, a closed short-let market for new entrants in the core, and a deeper international resale pool. Underwrite the residential outcome, verify any claimed short-let position, and treat tourist income as upside.

Both are real markets with real demand. The difference is which of the two policy directions you would rather own into.
