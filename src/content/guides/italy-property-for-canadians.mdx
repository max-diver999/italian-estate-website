**Quick answer:** Canada's reporting rules turn on how you use the Italian house rather than on what it cost. Kept for your own family, it is outside Form T1135 entirely. Listed for paying guests, it becomes specified foreign property and the switch is a change in use under subsection 45(1), which deems a sale at market value and taxes the accumulated gain in a year when no money has changed hands.

<TldrBlock text="Personal-use property is excluded from T1135 under para. 233.3(1)(b) · letting makes it reportable above CAD 100,000 · change of use under s.45(1) deems a sale at fair market value · s.45(2) election can defer it, by filed letter · CCA claims block the election"/>

Ask a Canadian owner what their Italian house costs them each year and they will name IMU, condominio charges and flights. The number that actually decides the outcome is none of those. It is whether the property is used personally or to earn income, because Canadian tax law treats those as two different assets and charges for the transition between them.

## Why is a personal holiday home invisible to the CRA?

Form T1135 captures specified foreign property once a taxpayer's total foreign holdings exceed CAD 100,000 in cost. The definition in paragraph 233.3(1)(b) carves out property used primarily for the personal use of the taxpayer or a person related to them.

A holiday house genuinely used by the family is exactly that carve-out. Its value is irrelevant: a €900,000 villa in Chianti used only by its owners is no more reportable than a €200,000 apartment in Ancona. The exclusion is about character, not size.

This runs against the instinct of Canadians who are used to value-based reporting thresholds, and it produces confident wrong answers in both directions. Owners of expensive personal-use properties file unnecessarily. Owners who rented for three weeks last summer do not file when they should.

- The test is primary use across the year, judged on the facts
- Related-party use counts as personal use, so lending the house to adult children does not change the character
- Cost amount rather than market value drives the CAD 100,000 aggregate threshold
- Nothing about the Italian side of the file records or reports any of this to Canada

## What exactly happens when the first guest pays?

The moment the property starts earning income the character changes, and Canadian law prices that change.

Subsection 45(1) treats a change in use from personal to income-producing as a disposition at fair market value followed by an immediate reacquisition at the same amount. No money moves and no deed is signed, but for tax purposes the owner has sold the house to themselves at today's value. Any appreciation since purchase crystallises as a capital gain, and the taxable half enters income for that year.

Consider what that means with real numbers. An apartment bought in 2018 for €280,000, worth €390,000 when the owner decides to list it, produces roughly €110,000 of gain on the change of use. Half is taxable. The owner has received nothing from anybody except a first booking that might be worth €900.

| Event | Amount | Canadian consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase in 2018 | €280,000 | Cost base established |
| Market value at change of use | €390,000 | Deemed proceeds |
| Deemed capital gain | €110,000 | Half taxable that year |
| Cash actually received | €900 booking | Does not fund the liability |
| New cost base going forward | €390,000 | Reduces the eventual real gain |

- **Red flag:** listing the property on a platform and then treating the first season as a trial. The trial is the taxable event
- The stepped-up cost base is real and does reduce tax on the eventual sale, so the charge is timing rather than pure cost
- A partial change of use, letting part of the property or part of the year, can produce a partial deemed disposition

## How does the section 45(2) election work, and when is it lost?

The Income Tax Act provides an election. Under subsection 45(2) the taxpayer may elect to be treated as not having changed the use, which defers the deemed disposition rather than eliminating it.

Three practical points decide whether it is available. It is made by filing a signed letter with the return for the year the use changed, so it depends on recognising the change at the time rather than in a later review. Claiming capital cost allowance on the property generally prevents relying on it, which matters because depreciation is often the first thing an accountant reaches for on a rental. And it is a deferral: the gain returns on a later actual disposition or when the election ceases to apply.

The decision framework is therefore about horizon. An owner who intends to let for two summers and then return the house to family use has a strong case for the election. An owner converting permanently to an income property may prefer the deemed disposition now, at today's value, if they expect the market to rise substantially afterwards.

- File the election with the return for the year of the change, not later
- Weigh the election against CCA, since taking one forecloses the other
- Where the property is jointly held, each owner's position is assessed separately

## What does the Italian side require once you let?

Canada decides the reporting. Italy decides whether you may let at all, and the Italian requirements have tightened considerably.

Every short-term letting needs a national CIN displayed on the listing and at the property, regional registration, and a SCIA filed with the comune once the activity crosses into professional territory. Municipal caps apply in the historic centres of several cities, and the condominium regolamento can prohibit tourist use outright regardless of what the comune permits. Our [holiday let licensing guide](/guides/italy-holiday-let-licensing/) sets out the layers, and the [Airbnb investment guide](/guides/airbnb-investment-italy-guide/) covers what the numbers look like once compliance is priced in.

The Italian tax follows at 21% on the first unit let or 26% beyond it under the flat regime, or under ordinary IRPEF with expenses, as our [cedolare secca guide](/guides/cedolare-secca-italy-rental-property/) explains. That Italian tax is creditable in Canada, so the income half of the picture behaves conventionally. It is only the change of use that has no Italian counterpart at all.

- Confirm CIN eligibility and the regolamento before the first listing, since a change of use for a letting you cannot legally run is the worst of both systems
- Italian tax paid is creditable, translated into Canadian dollars at transaction rates
- Keep the Italian filings, because the credit is evidenced rather than assumed

## Can an Italian house be your principal residence for Canadian purposes?

This is the part most Canadian owners have never been told, and for some families it is worth more than everything else on the page.

The principal residence exemption is not restricted to property in Canada. A housing unit qualifies where the taxpayer owns it and ordinarily inhabits it in the year, and nothing in that test requires the unit to be Canadian. A family can therefore designate their Italian house as principal residence for particular years, sheltering the gain attributable to those years from Canadian tax.

The constraint is that a family unit may designate only one property per year. Designating the Italian house for a year removes that year from the Canadian home's designation, so the exemption is being moved rather than duplicated. That makes it an allocation decision taken at the time of a sale, with the benefit going to whichever property appreciated more per year of ownership.

"Ordinarily inhabited" is a low bar in principle and a factual question in practice: seasonal occupation of a holiday home can satisfy it, since the rule contemplates properties lived in for part of the year. What it will not survive is a property that was let year-round and never occupied.

- The designation is made on the Canadian return in the year of disposition, not in advance
- Only one property per family unit per year, so the two houses compete for the same years
- Years where the Italian house was income-producing rather than inhabited generally cannot be designated
- Where the Italian property has appreciated faster than the Canadian one, the arithmetic can favour designating it despite the instinct that the family home comes first

## What valuation evidence does the change of use require?

If a deemed disposition happens, the number that fixes the tax is the fair market value on the day the use changed, and the taxpayer has to be able to support it years later.

That is harder for an Italian property than for a Canadian one. There is no municipal assessment to point to, the cadastral value is a fiscal figure far below market and is not evidence of value, and a Canadian accountant reviewing the file in 2032 will have no independent way to reconstruct what an apartment in Lecce was worth in 2026.

The answer is to create the evidence at the time. A written valuation from an Italian professional, a perizia, costs a few hundred euros and settles the question permanently. Two or three documented comparable sales from the same period are a weaker but usable alternative. Reconstructing a value retrospectively is where disputes and professional fees arise, and the cost of avoiding that is trivial against the tax at stake.

- Commission the valuation in the month the use changes, not in the year of the eventual sale
- Keep the Italian renovation invoices, since improvements raise the cost base on both the deemed and the real disposition
- Record the date the first booking was accepted, because that date and not the first stay is generally what marks the change

## Which Canadian buyer scenarios does this reshape?

**The family keeping it for themselves.** The simplest position in this entire cluster. No T1135, no change of use, no Canadian reporting on the property at all. The only Canadian obligations are on the Italian bank account if it grows large enough and on the eventual sale.

**The owner letting to cover costs.** The profile most likely to walk into the deemed disposition without noticing, because the letting feels incidental. If the intention is a few weeks a year, the section 45(2) election deserves consideration before the first booking rather than after.

**The buyer purchasing as an investment from the outset.** No change of use arises, because the property never had personal character to lose. The property is specified foreign property from acquisition and the reporting starts immediately, which is administratively heavier and free of the deemed-disposition trap.

**The owner converting a long-held family house.** The most exposed, because the deemed gain is proportional to how long the family has owned it. A house held since the 2000s can carry a gain large enough that the election is not merely preferable but necessary to make the letting viable at all.
