## Visura catastale: a tax record that is mistaken for a title deed

**Quick answer:** A visura catastale is an extract from the Italian cadastre. It tells you how the state classifies and taxes a property, and it does **not** prove who owns it. The cadastre is expressly non-probative: it names an intestatario, which is not the same thing as an owner. Ownership is established in a different register entirely, the registri immobiliari held by the Conservatoria.

Foreign buyers are shown a visura by an agent, see a name on it, and treat the question of title as answered. It is the most common documentary misunderstanding in an Italian purchase.

## What the document actually contains

A visura per immobile returns the identifiers and the fiscal classification, and each field does a job.

| Field | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Foglio, particella, subalterno | Sheet, parcel and unit numbers | The property's address in the cadastre. Garages and cellars carry their own subalterno |
| Categoria | Classification code, for example A/2 or A/3 | Decides tax treatment and whether the prima casa rate is available |
| Classe | Quality band inside the category | Feeds the rendita |
| Consistenza | Size, in rooms, cubic metres or square metres depending on category | Feeds the rendita |
| Rendita catastale | Notional annual income attributed to the property | The base from which most Italian property tax is calculated |
| Intestatario | Who the register shows | **Not** proof of ownership |

Three variants exist. A **visura per immobile** starts from the property. A **visura per soggetto** starts from a person and lists what the register attributes to them. A **visura storica** shows the history of changes, which is the one worth asking for when something looks odd.

## Why it does not prove ownership, and what does

The Italian cadastre was built to levy tax, not to establish title, and Italian law has never given it probative value on the question of who owns what. Courts say so plainly: a claim to ownership cannot rest on the cadastral record.

The practical reason is that the register goes stale. A property passes on death, or by gift, and the cadastral update is a separate step that heirs sometimes never take. The visura keeps naming a person who died years ago, or a parent who transferred the property long since. The document is accurate about the register and wrong about the world.

Ownership lives in the **registri immobiliari** at the Conservatoria, where every deed transferring or encumbering a real right is transcribed. That is where a notaio searches, and it is the search that matters. The relationship between the two registers is set out in [what signing the rogito achieves](/guides/rogito-italian-deed-of-sale/).

One more distinction is worth holding. A **visura** is an informal extract for consultation. A **certificato catastale** is issued with the signature and stamp of the Agenzia delle Entrate and certifies what the register says. The certificate has legal value as a certification; neither document turns a fiscal register into a title.

## The rendita catastale is the number your taxes come from

Almost every Italian property tax is calculated from the rendita rather than from what you paid.

**IMU**, the municipal tax on a second home, is charged on the cadastral value derived from the rendita, at a rate the comune sets between 0.86% and 1.06%. The mechanics are in [IMU and Italian property tax](/guides/imu-property-tax-italy/).

**Registration tax** on a resale from a private seller is charged on the cadastral value too, under the prezzo-valore mechanism, at 2% for a prima casa and 9% for a second home with a minimum of EUR 1,000. Because cadastral values sit well below market prices, a 9% headline rate frequently produces a smaller bill than a lower rate charged on the price would. That comparison, and the arithmetic behind it, is in [Italian registration tax](/guides/italy-registration-tax-property/).

A practical consequence follows. Two apparently similar flats at the same asking price can carry materially different annual and transaction costs, because their rendite differ. Reading the visura before you offer tells you which is which, and neither the listing nor the agent will volunteer it.

## Turning a rendita into the tax you will actually pay

The rendita is not itself the taxable base. It is revalued and then multiplied by a coefficient that depends on the category and on which tax you are calculating, and the same rendita produces three different bases.

Take a flat in category A/2 with a rendita of EUR 900.

| Purpose | Calculation | Base |
|---|---|---|
| Registration tax, prima casa | 900 × 1.05 × 110 | EUR 103,950 |
| Registration tax, second home | 900 × 1.05 × 120 | EUR 113,400 |
| IMU | 900 × 1.05 × 160 | EUR 151,200 |

Apply the rates and the annual and one-off costs fall out. At a comune rate of 1.06% the IMU on that flat is roughly EUR 1,602 a year. At the second-home registration rate of 9% the purchase tax is about EUR 10,206, whatever the flat actually sold for, so long as the buyer is an individual buying from a private seller and asks for the prezzo-valore treatment at the deed.

That last condition is the one foreign buyers miss. Prezzo-valore is not automatic. It has to be requested in the deed, and a buyer who does not request it is taxed on the price instead, which on a normal Italian resale is a substantially worse outcome.

Two further points make the arithmetic worth doing before you offer rather than after. A rendita fixed decades ago and never revised can sit far below what a comparable new-build carries, which is a real and lasting advantage attached to that specific unit. And a renovation that changes the category or the class raises the rendita, so a property bought cheaply and rebuilt handsomely comes with a higher standing tax bill than the visura you read at the start suggested.

## The categories that cost you the reduced rate

Three cadastral categories are treated as luxury and lose the prima casa benefit outright.

| Category | What it covers | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| A/1 | Signorial dwellings | No prima casa rate; IMU due even as a main residence |
| A/8 | Dwellings in villas | Same |
| A/9 | Castles and palaces of eminent historic or artistic value | Same |

The effect is large. A buyer expecting 2% registration tax on a prima casa pays 9% instead where the property sits in one of these categories, or 22% VAT rather than 4% when buying from a builder. On a substantial purchase the difference runs into six figures.

The classification parameters descend from a ministerial decree of 1969, and a property does not have to feel palatial to fall inside them. Anyone buying a period villa, a converted noble floor or anything described as signorile should read the category on the visura before modelling the tax, not after.

## The codes you are likely to meet

Residential property sits in group A, and the letter and number together tell you what the state thinks the property is.

| Code | What it designates |
|---|---|
| A/1 | Signorial dwelling, luxury for tax purposes |
| A/2 | Civil dwelling, the ordinary category for a decent flat or house |
| A/3 | Economic dwelling |
| A/7 | Small detached house or villino |
| A/8 | Dwelling in a villa, luxury for tax purposes |
| A/9 | Castle or palace of eminent artistic or historic value |
| A/10 | Private office, taxed on a different coefficient |
| C/2 | Storage, cellar or attic |
| C/6 | Garage or covered parking |
| C/7 | Open canopy or car port |

The C codes matter more than they look. A garage or cellar is usually a separate cadastral unit with its own subalterno and its own rendita, which means its own IMU. A buyer who read the rendita of the flat alone has understated the annual bill. It also means the garage can, in principle, be missing from the sale, so the deed has to list every subalterno being transferred.

One accompanying document is worth naming. The **voltura** is the update that reattributes the property to the new owner after a transfer, and it is the step that keeps the register from going stale. On a normal purchase the notaio handles it. On an inheritance nobody arranged, it is exactly the step that was skipped, which is why an out-of-date intestatario is such a common finding.

## Where the cadastre and reality part company

A second use of the visura, and of the **planimetria** that accompanies it, is checking that the registered property is the property standing there.

The deed requires a declaration that the cadastral data and the plan correspond to the actual state of the building. Mismatches are common: a wall moved, a terrace enclosed, a mezzanine added, a garage converted. Some are old and minor, some are unpermitted works with real consequences, and the cadastral plan is where the discrepancy first becomes visible on paper.

This is a job for a [geometra](/guides/geometra-role-italy-property/) rather than for a buyer with a tape measure. What matters is knowing that a clean visura says nothing about whether the layout matches, and that a mismatch discovered after the compromesso is expensive rather than merely irritating. The wider check sequence is in [due diligence before the compromesso](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/), and the relationship between cadastral and market values in [cadastral value against market price](/guides/cadastral-value-vs-market-price-italy/).

## Getting hold of one

Access is open, which surprises buyers used to systems where property records are closed.

The Agenzia delle Entrate issues visure online and at its counters. Owners can pull records on their own property free of charge through the agency's online services. Records on property you do not own are available for a modest fee, which is what makes the document useful to a buyer at the point where it matters, before an offer rather than after acceptance.

In practice a foreign buyer rarely does this alone. A geometra, a notaio or an instructed lawyer pulls the visura and the planimetria as a matter of routine, and the cost of the documents is trivial next to the cost of misreading them. What you should insist on is seeing them, in the original, early. An agent describing a property as A/2 is not the same as a visura saying so, and the difference between A/2 and A/8 is measured in tens of thousands of euro.

Ask for three things together: the visura per immobile for every subalterno in the sale, the planimetria, and a visura storica. The first two give you the position, and the third gives you the changes, which is where anything irregular usually shows first. If a seller or an agent treats that request as unusual, it is worth pausing over: the documents cost a few euro, they are issued by a state agency, and every serious Italian buyer asks for them as a matter of course.

## Weighing what the visura is worth

**What it tells you reliably**

- The property's cadastral identity, down to the individual garage or cellar
- The category, and therefore whether the reduced purchase rate is available at all
- The rendita, and therefore the annual and transaction tax you should model
- The registered layout, as a plan to compare against what you walked through
- The history of changes, where a visura storica is requested

**What it cannot tell you**

- Who owns the property, which requires a Conservatoria search
- Whether anything is registered against it, which requires the same search
- Whether the works were permitted, which the cadastre does not record
- Whether the layout on file matches the building, which needs a professional to compare
- Whether a neighbour has acquired rights over part of it by long use

The proportionate reading is that the visura is indispensable and insufficient. Pull it early, because it costs almost nothing and decides your tax model. Then treat the ownership question as open until the register that actually answers it has been searched.
