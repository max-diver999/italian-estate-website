## Italy digital nomad visa: what it is, and who it actually refuses

**Quick answer:** Italy's digital nomad route opened on 5 April 2024 and covers two groups: self-employed people working for clients outside Italy, and employees of a non-Italian company working remotely. Both must qualify as highly specialised workers, which is the requirement that decides most applications. The legal income floor is about EUR 24,790 a year; consulates in practice ask between EUR 25,500 and EUR 28,000.

Most English-language guides to this visa reproduce the same list of documents and one income figure. The list is not where applications fail. They fail on a qualification test that is easy to miss, and on the fact that consulates apply the same decree differently depending on which one has jurisdiction over you.

## Where the visa comes from, and why that matters

The route was created by an inter-ministerial decree dated 29 February 2024 and in force from 5 April 2024, and its operational detail was settled by a later implementing decree signed jointly by the Ministers of the Interior, Labour and Foreign Affairs. In 2026 it was formally folded into the Immigration Code rather than sitting alongside it.

That history explains an oddity applicants keep running into. Because the decree set principles and left consulates to implement them, the published requirements are not identical from one consulate to the next. The law is national; the paperwork is local. An applicant reading a guide written from the New York consulate's page and applying through London will find requirements that do not match.

There is one structural advantage worth understanding before anything else. This route sits **outside the decreto flussi**, the annual quota system that caps most Italian work immigration, and it does not require the employment clearance those routes need. For anyone who has tried to enter Italy through an ordinary self-employment visa and found the quota exhausted within hours of opening, that is the whole point of the category.

## The two categories, and why yours may not exist at your consulate

The decree covers two distinct people:

**Digital nomads** are self-employed. Freelancers, independent consultants, people invoicing clients from their own business, all working remotely with clients outside Italy.

**Remote workers** are employees. They hold a contract with a company that is not Italian and perform that work from Italy.

Both are covered by the same decree. Consular practice, however, has diverged. The Italian Consulate General in London lists this route as being **for self-employed applicants only**. The Consulate General in New York publishes it as a combined **digital nomad and remote worker** visa. Both are implementing the same national rule.

The practical consequence is direct: your consulate is determined by where you legally reside, not by where you would prefer to apply, and its published position determines what you can file. A salaried remote employee living in the United Kingdom and a salaried remote employee living in the United States are, on paper, in the same position under Italian law and in different positions in front of the counter.

Check your own consulate's page as the first step of the process rather than the last. Where its position is unclear, the question to put in writing is narrow: does this consulate accept applications from employees of a foreign company under the digital nomad and remote worker route, or from self-employed applicants only.

## The requirement that decides most applications

Income gets the attention. The **highly specialised worker** test does the refusing.

Both categories must meet it. In Italian immigration practice the concept generally means a university degree relevant to the work being performed, or several years of documented professional experience at a comparable level. It is the same concept used for other skilled routes, and it is not a formality.

This is what separates the digital nomad visa from the popular idea of it. A person earning EUR 40,000 a year through general remote administrative work, with no degree in the field and no documented specialist track record, meets the income requirement comfortably and does not meet this one. A software engineer earning EUR 30,000 with a relevant degree does the opposite.

Alongside it sits a second qualification requirement that is easier to satisfy but frequently overlooked: **at least six months of prior experience** in the type of work you intend to perform in Italy. New freelancers and career changers are the applicants this catches.

## What the income requirement actually is

The legal floor is set by reference rather than by a fixed number. It is **three times the annual exemption threshold for healthcare contributions**, which works out at approximately **EUR 24,790 a year**, or about EUR 2,066 a month.

Published consular practice sits above it. Figures between **EUR 25,500 and EUR 28,000** are commonly requested, while the New York consulate has continued to reference EUR 24,789. The spread is not a contradiction so much as a margin: consulates are entitled to satisfy themselves that the income is stable, and asking for a figure above the statutory minimum is how they do it.

Three things follow for anyone modelling this.

Apply against **your consulate's published figure**, not the decree's. If it publishes nothing, plan on the upper end of the range rather than the floor.

Expect to prove **stability, not just amount**. A single strong month proves nothing. Twelve months of bank statements, filed accounts, or a contract with a term left to run are what carry an application.

The income must come from **outside Italy**. Clients or an employer based in Italy do not count toward the threshold and, for the self-employed category, an Italian client base starts to look like an Italian business rather than remote work.

| The number | What it is | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| About EUR 24,790 a year | The legal floor | Three times the annual healthcare exemption threshold |
| About EUR 2,066 a month | The same figure, monthly | Arithmetic on the above |
| EUR 25,500 to EUR 28,000 | What consulates commonly ask | Published consular practice, varies by post |
| EUR 24,789 | Still referenced by New York | That consulate's published page |

## What you file, and in what order

The document set is unremarkable. Getting it in the right order is what shortens the process.

Before the appointment, assemble proof of the remote work arrangement itself: a contract with a non-Italian employer, or client contracts and invoices if self-employed. Add evidence of the qualification, meaning the degree certificate or a documented professional history, and evidence of the six months of prior experience. Add twelve months of financial history rather than a snapshot. Add health insurance valid in Italy for the full period, and proof of accommodation, which can be a registered lease or a deed if you already own.

Then the sequence runs: consular appointment, visa issued, entry to Italy, and within eight days of arrival the application to the **questura** for the residence permit. The permit is issued for a **maximum of one year** and renews year to year while the original conditions still hold. There is no multi-year permit at first issue, so the renewal file matters as much as the application file.

The one Italian administrative step that catches everybody is the **codice fiscale**, the tax code. Nothing works without it: not a lease, not a bank account, not a utility contract. Obtain it early rather than at the point it blocks something.

## The tax question, answered properly

This is where guides written to sell a service tend to go quiet.

Italian tax residence is triggered by spending **more than 183 days** in Italy in a calendar year, and an Italian tax resident is taxed on **worldwide income**. The digital nomad visa is designed for people who live in Italy. Tax residence is therefore the expected outcome of using it as intended, not an edge case to be managed around.

That has consequences a prospective applicant should model before applying rather than after arriving.

Your income becomes taxable in Italy even though the payer is abroad. Where your own country also taxes it, relief depends on the double taxation treaty between the two, and treaties differ on how they treat self-employment income against employment income. United States citizens face the additional complication of citizenship-based taxation, which no treaty removes entirely.

There are preferential regimes for people moving their tax residence to Italy, and whether any of them fits a nomad's circumstances is a question for a commercialista rather than a website. The starting point is [the flat tax regime for new residents](/guides/italy-flat-tax-regime-new-residents/), which is built for a different income profile but sets out how the elections work.

The practical advice is narrow and worth following: get a written opinion on your specific tax position before you file the visa application. It is far cheaper than discovering the answer in year two.

## What the tax actually costs at nomad income levels

Once resident, remote income is taxed through IRPEF on the ordinary scale. The 2026 budget law, Legge 30 dicembre 2025 n. 199, cut the middle band, so the figures below are current rather than the ones most guides still reproduce.

| Taxable income | Rate in 2026 | Was |
|---|---|---|
| Up to EUR 28,000 | 23% | unchanged |
| EUR 28,001 to EUR 50,000 | **33%** | 35% before 2026 |
| Above EUR 50,000 | 43% | unchanged |

The reduction to 33% applies only up to EUR 200,000 of income, and the maximum it saves anyone is about EUR 440 a year, so it changes a monthly budget rather than a decision.

One alignment is worth noticing. The visa's income floor of roughly EUR 24,790 sits below the top of the first band, so an applicant qualifying at or near the minimum is taxed entirely at 23% before regional and municipal surcharges. Those surcharges are set locally and typically add between 1% and 3%, which is one of the few genuine cost differences between living in one comune and another.

Add social contributions on top. The rate depends on the category and on whether a treaty assigns them elsewhere, and it is the line that most often turns an attractive gross figure into an unremarkable net one. The 183-day threshold that triggers all of this is measured across the calendar year, so arriving in July and arriving in May put you in different tax years entirely.

## How it compares with the other two routes

Three Italian routes are routinely confused, and they suit different people.

| | Digital nomad visa | [Elective residence](/guides/italy-elective-residence-visa-property/) | [Investor visa](/guides/italy-golden-visa/) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Built for | People who work remotely | People who live on passive income | People placing capital |
| May you work? | Yes, that is the point | No, strictly prohibited | Not required, global business allowed |
| Income or capital test | About EUR 24,790 a year from remote work | Passive income, EUR 31,160 upward in practice | EUR 250,000 to EUR 2,000,000 invested |
| Qualification gate | Highly specialised worker | None of this kind | Source of funds |
| Permit at first issue | One year | One year | Two years |
| Realistic for a salaried remote worker | Yes, subject to consulate | No, work is prohibited | No, unless separately wealthy |

The distinction that matters most: **elective residence forbids work of any kind**, including remote work for a foreign employer. Applicants who intend to keep working and apply for elective residence because it looks easier are applying for the wrong visa, and the prohibition is enforced at renewal.

## Where applications actually fail

Four patterns account for most refusals and delays.

**Qualification, not income.** The applicant meets the money test and cannot evidence the specialisation. This is the single most common cause and the hardest to fix late, because a degree or a documented track record either exists or does not.

**Income proved as a snapshot.** A bank balance on one day rather than a year of flow. Consulates are assessing whether the income will continue, and a balance does not answer that question.

**The wrong consulate, or the wrong category at the right one.** A remote employee applying through a post that publishes the route as self-employed only.

**Accommodation and insurance treated as afterthoughts.** Both are hard requirements. A hotel booking is not accommodation and travel insurance is not health cover for the period of stay.

## If the plan includes buying rather than renting

Nothing about this visa requires property ownership, and a registered lease satisfies the accommodation requirement perfectly well. Most people who move on it rent first, which is the sensible order.

Where a purchase is the plan, two points are worth separating. Ownership in Italy is governed by [reciprocity rules](/guides/italy-reciprocity-property-foreigners/) rather than by any visa, so buying is a separate question from residing. And the practical side of choosing a base as a remote worker, meaning connectivity, coworking, and which cities actually function year round, is covered in our [property guide for digital nomads](/guides/digital-nomad-italy-property-guide/), alongside [what a month in Italy costs](/guides/cost-of-living-italy/).

## What it costs to run, beyond the application

The visa fee itself is minor next to the recurring obligations, and applicants routinely budget only for the former.

Health insurance valid in Italy for the whole permit period is a hard requirement at application and again at renewal, and travel policies do not satisfy it. Once tax resident, social contributions follow the category: a self-employed applicant registers with the Italian system and pays contributions on declared income, while an employee of a foreign company sits in a more complicated position that depends on the treaty and on whether the employer registers in Italy at all.

Then there is the accountant. A commercialista is not optional on this route in the way it might be for a tourist buying a holiday flat, because the annual declaration has to reconcile foreign income against Italian residence every year the permit runs. Treat that as a fixed annual cost from the first year rather than a one-off at the start, and see [what a month in Italy costs](/guides/cost-of-living-italy/) for the household side of the same budget.
