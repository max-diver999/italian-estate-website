## The Molise housing market, and what half the national price actually buys

**Quick answer:** Molise asks EUR 1,036 to EUR 1,139 per square metre, against a national average near EUR 2,188. It is the cheapest mainland region in Italy and the least visited, with roughly 35 kilometres of Adriatic coast and two provinces. The discount is not a mispricing waiting to correct: it reflects a small population, thin transport, and a resale market that is almost entirely domestic.

That does not make it a bad purchase. It makes it a purchase to enter for use rather than for exit, and the distinction decides almost everything else.

## Two provinces, one short coast

Molise divides into **Campobasso**, which holds the regional capital of the same name and the entire coastline, and **Isernia**, which is inland, mountainous and cheaper than anywhere else on the Italian mainland.

Campobasso city is inland, on a hill, and works as a small administrative and university centre rather than as a destination. Its tenant base comes from the regional hospital, the public administration and the Università degli Studi del Molise, which between them keep letting demand steady across the year in a way no coastal town here manages.

The coast is the region's scarcest asset. **Termoli** is the only town of size on it, a walled old town on a promontory with a working port and ferries to the Tremiti islands, and **Campomarino** sits just north of it. Thirty-five kilometres is very little coastline, and the pricing reflects that scarcity directly.

**Isernia province** is the value floor: hill villages, national park edges, and a lot of stone houses that have been empty for a generation.

## What the market asks

| Where | Asking, per square metre | Monthly rent, per square metre |
|---|---|---|
| Molise, region-wide | EUR 1,036 to 1,139 | about EUR 7.34 across the province |
| Campobasso city | about EUR 1,085 | about EUR 6.97 |
| Termoli | about EUR 1,861 | about EUR 9.67 |
| Campomarino | not separately quoted | about EUR 11.54 |
| Isernia province | about EUR 669 | thin, largely unlet |
| Italy, for comparison | about EUR 2,188 | national reference |

Three things stand out. Termoli is nearly three times Isernia province, which is a very wide spread for a region this small. Campomarino's rents run above Termoli's despite the town being smaller, because they are seasonal rather than residential. And Campobasso, the capital, asks less than the regional coastal town by a wide margin, which is unusual and tells you the coast is being priced on scarcity rather than on income.

## The three positions people actually take

**Campobasso, for year-round letting.** Furnished twelve-month leases to hospital staff, university tenants and public-sector employees return 4% to 5.5% gross on tickets under EUR 180,000. This is the only part of Molise where a rental position does not depend on a season, and it is correspondingly dull. Nothing about it will appreciate quickly.

**Termoli, for the coast.** Short letting here reaches 5% to 6% gross in a good season, on assumptions that have to be stated honestly: 65% to 75% occupancy across the peak, a winter void of 40% to 50% across the year, and management at 25% to 35% of gross where the owner is not present. A dual strategy, short lets in summer and a long lease over winter, works better here than in most places, because the town has a genuine off-season population.

**Isernia province, for the house itself.** At about EUR 669 per square metre, and below EUR 500 in peripheral comuni, this is renovation territory. A rustico under EUR 100,000 is a real thing here. So is the cost of making it habitable, the car you will need, and the compliance position on works nobody permitted decades ago. This is also where Molise's cheap-house schemes concentrate, and [the one euro houses programme](/guides/1-euro-houses-italy/) sets out what those actually commit a buyer to.

## The illiquidity, stated plainly

Molise's resale market is the thinnest on the Italian mainland. The buyer pool for an inland property is domestic, small, and not growing. A mispriced hill property can sit for eighteen months without a serious offer, and the price it eventually achieves is set by a handful of local comparables rather than by any national trend.

Two practical consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

First, buy what you would be content to own for a long time. An exit strategy that depends on selling within three years is not one this market supports.

Second, use the illiquidity when buying rather than only fearing it. A thin market cuts both ways: the same shortage of buyers that will slow your exit is the reason the seller in front of you has few alternatives today. Ask how long a property has been listed, and anchor to closed OMI comparables in the same comune rather than to the asking price. In a market with few transactions, the asking price carries very little information.

## Reaching it, and living with the distances

Molise has no airport. Arrivals come through Naples, roughly two hours from Campobasso by road, or Rome, closer to three, or Pescara in neighbouring Abruzzo for the seasonal routes. The Adriatic rail line stops at Termoli, which puts the coast within reach of Bologna and Bari without a car; everywhere else in the region assumes one.

That single fact does more to shape the market than any price figure. Termoli functions as a place a visitor can arrive at and stay in. An Isernia hill village functions as a place you drive to, which narrows both the rental pool and the resale pool to people who have already decided they want to be there.

Winters inland are colder and longer than the Adriatic latitude suggests, because the interior climbs quickly. Campobasso sits above 700 metres and gets snow. A stone house bought in July for its shade reads differently in February, and heating costs on unrenovated rural stock are the running expense buyers most often underestimate.

## What the region is like, since almost nobody has been

Molise is the punchline of a long-running Italian joke about not existing, and the reason is simply that very few people pass through it. It has no motorway spine of its own, no famous city, and a population smaller than a mid-sized Italian suburb.

The consequence for a buyer is that almost nothing here has been priced by outside demand, which is unusual in Italy and is the whole basis of the discount. What the region does have is the shape of central Italy without the traffic: the Matese and Mainarde mountains, transhumance trails still marked across the interior, Samnite and Roman remains at Saepinum and Pietrabbondante that would carry entrance queues anywhere else, and a food culture built on sheep, pasta and not much money.

The commercial reading of that is neither romantic nor dismissive. Scarcity of visitors means scarcity of the demand that supports short-let returns, which is exactly why the coastal strip prices at three times the interior. It also means an unrenovated village house here is cheap because nobody is competing for it, not because something is wrong with it.

For a buyer, the sorting question is honest and simple. If the property is to be used, Molise offers more house per euro than anywhere else on the mainland and a genuinely intact rural Italy behind it. If the property is to be let, only Campobasso and the Termoli strip support the case, and the rest of the region does not.

## Against Abruzzo and Marche

Molise asks 25% to 35% below neighbouring [Abruzzo](/guides/abruzzo-property-investment-guide/), which averages near EUR 1,192 per square metre. On the coast the gap is narrower in percentage terms but wider in what it buys: Pescara at about EUR 2,140 is a real city with an airport and a university, while Termoli at about EUR 1,861 is a handsome town of modest size.

Marche, further north, averages near EUR 1,499 with Ancona at about EUR 1,541, and has employment depth that neither of the smaller regions matches.

The honest ranking for a foreign buyer is that Marche offers the deepest market, Abruzzo the best balance of price and infrastructure, and Molise the lowest entry with the least behind it. Anyone choosing Molise over the other two should be doing so for the specific property, not for the region's prospects.

## Buying costs and finance

A non-resident buying a second home should budget **10% to 15%** of the price again in transaction costs. Registration tax at **9%** is the largest line, and it is charged on cadastral value rather than on the price agreed, which in a region this cheap produces a genuinely small absolute bill. Rental income on a furnished long lease is taxed at a flat **21%** under cedolare secca. The detail sits in [the cost of buying property in Italy](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/).

Financing is the harder part. Italian banks lend to non-residents against a valuation rather than a price, typically at **50% to 60%**, and in low-transaction markets valuations come in conservative. On a EUR 90,000 rustico the mortgage market is largely theoretical: below roughly EUR 100,000 most lenders are not interested at all, and purchases at that level are cash.

## What to establish before committing

| Check | Why it matters more here than elsewhere |
|---|---|
| Building compliance on the whole property, outbuildings included | Rural Molise stock frequently carries decades of unpermitted work |
| The cadastral position against what stands there | Divergences are the rule rather than the exception on old rustici |
| Access rights and boundaries on rural parcels | Often established by long use rather than by deed |
| Seismic classification and the structural history | Much of the region is classified, and the neighbouring 2009 quake reset local practice |
| Whether the property has mains water, drainage and a usable road | Not assumed at these prices |
| How long the listing has been live, and closed comparables in the comune | The asking price carries little information in a thin market |

The compliance items are the ones that stop deeds rather than merely cost money, and the sequence for checking them is in [due diligence before the compromesso](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/). The eligibility position for a foreign buyer is in [buying property in Italy as a foreigner](/guides/buy-property-italy-foreigner/), and the yield arithmetic in [the Italian rental yield guide](/guides/italy-rental-yield-guide/). Within the region, the comparison most buyers make is set out in [Campobasso against Termoli](/compare/campobasso-vs-termoli-property/).

## Advantages and disadvantages

### Advantages

- The lowest entry on the Italian mainland, at roughly half the national average per square metre
- Isernia province below EUR 700 per square metre, with peripheral comuni under EUR 500
- Registration tax charged on cadastral value, which makes the absolute purchase tax very small
- Steady year-round letting in Campobasso from hospital, university and administrative tenants
- A short, scarce coastline that has held its relative value better than the interior
- Almost no international competition for stock, which keeps negotiation genuinely open

### Disadvantages

- The thinnest resale market on the mainland, with a domestic and shrinking buyer pool
- No airport of its own, and limited rail beyond the coastal line
- A winter void of 40% to 50% on any coastal letting position
- Mortgage finance effectively unavailable below roughly EUR 100,000
- Rural stock frequently carrying unpermitted works and incomplete cadastral records
- Seismic classification across much of the region, adding survey cost to cheap purchases
