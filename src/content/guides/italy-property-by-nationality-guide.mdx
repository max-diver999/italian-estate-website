**Quick answer:** Italian law asks one question about your nationality and then stops asking. Article 16 of the preliminary provisions to the Civil Code makes a non-EU citizen's capacity to buy conditional on reciprocity, verified by the notary against Foreign Ministry tables and read from the passport rather than the residence permit. Everything else that varies between a German, a Canadian and an Israeli buyer is written in their own country's tax code.

<TldrBlock text="One legal test: reciprocity, art. 16 preleggi · read from citizenship, not residence · EU, EEA, Swiss and Italian permit holders exempt · failure voids the deed · everything else differs at home, not in Italy" />

This page exists to separate two things that get bundled together. There is a genuine legal question about nationality in Italian property law, and it is narrow. There is a much larger set of differences between buyers of different nationalities, and none of it is Italian.

## What is the reciprocity condition, and who does it actually catch?

Article 16 of the preliminary provisions to the Civil Code conditions a foreign national's civil capacity on whether their own state extends comparable rights to Italians. For property that means a straightforward question: could an Italian citizen buy a house in your country on broadly similar terms? The Ministry of Foreign Affairs publishes tables country by country, the notary consults them, and the answer is recorded before the deed.

The test catches fewer people than its reputation suggests. Reciprocity is presumed satisfied for EU and EEA citizens, for Swiss citizens under the free movement agreement signed in 1999, and for stateless persons and refugees resident in Italy for over 3 years. It also falls away for any non-EU national holding an Italian residence permit for work, family reasons, study or long-term residence, because at that point residence in Italy answers the question instead.

What remains is the non-EU citizen buying from abroad with no Italian permit, and there the answer depends on which passport is on the table. Our [reciprocity guide](/guides/italy-reciprocity-property-foreigners/) works through how the tables read and where the limits are partial rather than absolute, since some entries permit urban residential purchases while restricting agricultural land.

- The check belongs to the notary and happens before the deed, not at the offer
- A failure is not a delay: without capacity the deed is void and ownership does not pass
- Partial entries exist, so a table that says yes for an apartment can say no for farmland

The instruments are old and short, which is worth knowing before treating the question as bureaucratic. Article 16 sits in the preliminary provisions enacted with the Civil Code in 1942 and runs to two paragraphs. The exemption for foreign nationals holding an Italian residence permit comes from the immigration consolidated act, legislative decree 286 of 1998, at article 2. The 3 years of residence that qualify a refugee or stateless person come from the same body of rules. Nothing here has been rewritten in the modern era, which is why the tables rather than the statute carry the day-to-day answer.

| Instrument | Year | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| Art. 16 preleggi, Civil Code | 1942 | The reciprocity condition itself |
| D.Lgs. 286, art. 2 | 1998 | Permit holders and equal treatment |
| EU Succession Regulation 650 | 2012 | Which law governs the estate, by choice or residence |
| Schengen Borders Code stay limit | 90 days in 180 | How long a third-country owner may stay |

## Why does the passport decide it and not the residence permit?

Because capacity under article 16 attaches to nationality, and the Gulf is where this matters most. An Indian or Pakistani national who has lived in Dubai for fifteen years, banks there and pays no income tax there is still assessed against India or Pakistan. A British national resident in Abu Dhabi is assessed as British. The Emirates ID answers a question Italian law is not asking.

Two consequences follow, and both are practical rather than theoretical. A dual national may rely on whichever of their nationalities clears the table, which occasionally makes a second passport the fastest route to a clean file. And a buyer whose reciprocity position is doubtful has a second path that does not depend on the tables at all: an Italian residence permit, which substitutes residence for the reciprocity test.

| Buyer | What the notary checks | Result |
|---|---|---|
| French citizen in Paris | Nothing. EU citizen | Buys freely |
| Swiss citizen in Zurich | Nothing. Free movement agreement | Buys freely |
| American citizen in Texas | Reciprocity table, United States | Satisfied |
| Indian citizen resident in Dubai | Reciprocity table, India | Assessed on India |
| Non-EU national with Italian work permit | The permit | Reciprocity not required |

- **Insider tip:** if you hold two passports, tell the notary both at the first document request rather than after a table check goes badly
- Residence in a third country neither helps nor hurts the reciprocity question
- The [purchase sequence](/guides/how-to-buy-italy-property-step-by-step/) is identical once capacity is established

## How do you read a reciprocity entry, and what does a partial answer mean?

The tables are organised by country and by subject, and the property entry is rarely a plain yes. A typical entry confirms that Italian citizens may acquire urban real estate in that state, then qualifies it: agricultural land restricted, land within a defined distance of a border or a military installation excluded, ownership permitted only through a locally registered company. Italian practice mirrors the qualification back, so a buyer from a state that limits Italians to urban residential property is themselves limited to urban residential property in Italy.

That mirroring produces the results people find surprising. A buyer whose passport clears for an apartment in Milan can be refused on a farmhouse with attached land in Umbria, and the refusal is not discretionary. Where a purchase includes agricultural parcels, and many rural Italian sales do, the cadastral classification of every parcel in the deed matters as much as the building.

- Ask which parcels are in the sale and how each is classified before assuming the table's answer covers the whole property
- Where the answer is partial, restructuring the sale to separate parcels is sometimes possible and always the notary's call
- Table entries are updated by the Ministry rather than by statute, so an answer from 2 years ago is worth re-checking

## What happens when the buyer is a company rather than a person?

Reciprocity attaches to legal persons too, and the question shifts from the passport to the place of incorporation. A company incorporated in an EU member state buys on the same terms as an Italian company. A company incorporated outside the EU is assessed against its own state's treatment of Italian companies, which is a different table entry from the one covering individuals and occasionally a different answer.

This creates a practical asymmetry worth knowing before structuring. An American individual and a Delaware LLC are two different applicants under article 16, and the LLC's route can be the slower one even where both ultimately clear. The [company purchase guide](/guides/buying-property-italy-through-company/) covers what an Italian or foreign corporate buyer changes about tax and financing, which is a larger question than capacity; capacity is only the first gate.

| Buyer type | What is assessed | Common outcome |
|---|---|---|
| EU individual | Nothing | Free |
| Non-EU individual, no Italian permit | Reciprocity table for their citizenship | Usually satisfied, sometimes partial |
| Non-EU individual with Italian permit | The permit | Reciprocity not required |
| EU-incorporated company | Nothing | Free |
| Non-EU company | Reciprocity for legal persons | Assessed separately from individuals |

- **Red flag:** an agent who tells a non-EU corporate buyer that reciprocity is a formality has not read the entry for legal persons
- The company's shareholders' nationality is not the test; the place of incorporation is
- Where capacity is doubtful for a company, an Italian holding company is the usual answer and carries its own tax consequences

## What does not change with nationality, despite what buyers expect?

The list is longer than the list of things that do change. Registration tax is 9% of cadastral value on a second home and 2% with prima casa relief regardless of passport. The mortgage market treats a non-resident as a non-resident, and an Italian bank lending to a German non-resident applies the same loan-to-value band it applies to an Australian one. The [codice fiscale](/guides/codice-fiscale-italy-property/) is issued to everyone on the same terms. Inheritance follows the deceased's habitual residence or their chosen national law under the EU succession regulation, which is a choice available to non-EU nationals too.

Even the short-let rules are national rather than nationality-based: the CIN requirement, the comune's own caps, the condominium regolamento. A Dutch owner and a Canadian owner letting the same apartment face the same Italian obligations and the same Italian rates, 21% on the first unit let and 26% on the second.

Residence obligations are also blind to the passport. Prima casa relief requires registering residence in the comune within 18 months of the deed for every buyer who claims it, and the recovery of the relief with penalties applies identically to a French buyer and an Emirati one.

This matters editorially, and it is why the country pages here were rewritten. A page that repeats Italian registration tax under twelve different flags is not twelve pages, it is one page with twelve titles, and it tells a reader nothing they could not get from the [tax comparison](/guides/italy-prima-casa-vs-second-home-tax/) directly.

## What genuinely differs, and where is it written?

The real differences sit in the buyer's home tax system, and they change decisions rather than paperwork. A handful of examples, each covered in full on its own page:

- **Germany** exempts Italian rental income from German tax and, because Italy is in the EU, does not even use it to raise the German rate on other income
- **The United States** taxes its citizens on worldwide income and treats a euro-denominated mortgage as a separate currency transaction from the house it financed
- **Canada** makes the reporting obligation turn on use: a personal holiday home is invisible, and the first paid booking changes that
- **Denmark, Norway and Sweden** diverge completely on whether the house is taxed annually at home, which is why one Nordic page cannot serve all three
- **Switzerland** exempts the Italian house but allocates mortgage interest across the whole portfolio, so buying in Italy reduces the interest deductible in Switzerland
- **Ireland** has no succession treaty with Italy, so Italy's generous family exemption produces no Irish credit at all

None of these is an Italian rule. All of them decide whether a purchase makes sense.

## Which buyer scenarios should send you to which page?

A short decision framework, since the useful sorting is by tax system rather than by passport.

**You file taxes where you live and hold that country's passport.** Read the page for that country. The Italian side is the same everywhere; the page exists for the home-country half.

**Your citizenship and your tax residence are in different countries.** Read the reciprocity table for your citizenship and the tax page for where you file. These are genuinely separate questions and conflating them is the most common error in this cluster.

**You are moving to Italy rather than buying a second home.** Nationality stops being the organising question. Residence, the flat tax regimes and prima casa relief take over, and the country pages will not be the most useful thing here.

**You hold a passport with no page on this site.** The [foreigner's purchase guide](/guides/buy-property-italy-foreigner/) covers every Italian requirement, and the reciprocity table covers the one thing your nationality decides. A cross-border accountant in your own jurisdiction covers the rest, and that is the correct division of labour rather than a hedge.
