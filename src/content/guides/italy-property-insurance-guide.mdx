Quick answer: Italian home insurance is cheap, modular and mostly optional, which is why only 7.3% of homes carry catastrophe cover in one of Europe's most seismic countries: the bank's required fire policy costs €150-350 a year, a full multirischi package €250-900, and quake or flood extensions €300-1,200 by hazard. For a foreign owner the rational stack is three layers (structure, liability, catastrophe by zone), priced against rebuild cost rather than market value, and reviewed once against the condominio's building policy. This guide covers the covers, the premiums, the claims process and the four classic mistakes, with the seismic-zone logic detailed in the [earthquake guide](/guides/seismic-zones-italy-property/).

## What insurance does an Italian home legally need?

The legal baseline is nearly empty: no national law obliges a private owner to insure a home, the 2025 catastrophe mandate binds firms (with staggered deadlines through 31 December 2025), and the single compulsory case a foreign buyer meets is the fire-and-explosion policy a mortgage lender requires on its collateral, priced €150-350 a year on typical stock.

| Obligation | Who it binds | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Incendio e scoppio (fire policy) | mortgage borrowers, on the bank's requirement | €150-350/yr or single premium |
| Cat-nat mandate 2025 | businesses with Italian legal seats | n/a for private homes |
| Condominio globale fabbricati | the building, voted by assembly | inside your [spese](/guides/condominio-rules-foreign-owners/) |
| Everything else | nobody | optional by design |

Two consequences follow from that emptiness. Cover is a buyer's market: insurers compete on modular packages with little adverse selection, which keeps Italian premiums low against comparable European risk. And protection gaps are the norm: the same optionality that keeps prices friendly leaves 92.7% of homes exposed to quake and flood, a statistic that should read as opportunity, not reassurance, to the owner of a six-figure asset 1,000 km from their primary residence.

Penetration numbers deserve one paragraph of context because they are the strangest fact in European insurance: ANIA's 2025 figures put homes covered against quake and flood at 7.3% in a country where 38% of comuni sit in seismic zones 1-2 and flood maps colour whole river valleys, against catastrophe take-up rates above 50% in comparable markets. The historical explanation is the state's role as reinsurer of last resort: 8 decades of post-disaster reconstruction aid, from Irpinia's decades-long programmes to L'Aquila's rebuild, taught households that the treasury eventually pays. The 2025 firm-side mandate, deadlines staggered 31 March to 31 December by company size, was the first legislative break with that model, and the household market's direction of travel reads clearly enough from it: voluntary today, priced for a mandated tomorrow.

Buyer scenarios set the starting stack: the mortgage buyer inherits the fire policy and should add liability plus water on day 1; the cash buyer of a lakefront apartment starts from zero and assembles the full multirischi; the zone 1-2 rural buyer prices the quake extension in the same breath as the purchase itself.

**Insider tip:** if a bank sells you its fire policy at the rogito, note the cancellation rules: borrowers may switch insurers on mortgage-linked policies, and bank-channel premiums routinely run 30-50% above identical broker-market cover. The €200 saved annually funds half a proper multirischi.

## What does a multirischi policy actually cover?

The multirischi casa is the market's standard package, and its architecture is modular: a fire-and-natural-events core, then water damage, theft, glass, third-party liability, legal protection and assistance modules attached by choice, with premiums assembling from €250-600 on apartments and €400-900 on villas at ordinary sums insured.

| Module | Covers | Foreign-owner notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fire + events core | fire, explosion, lightning, storm | the bank's minimum lives here |
| Water damage | burst pipes, seepage from your systems | the most-claimed module in Italy |
| Theft + valuables | contents, with sub-limits | second homes: check occupancy clauses |
| RC (liability) | damage your property causes others | the module you must not skip |
| Legal protection | disputes, counsel costs | useful for letting owners |
| Assistance | emergency trades, locksmiths | valuable at 1,000 km distance |

Module pricing rewards comparison shopping precisely because the architecture is standard: 3 quotes on identical modules and sums routinely spread 30-40% on the same Como asset, brokers place across companies at no visible cost to you, and the multirischi market's competitiveness is the one place where Italy's low insurance penetration works entirely in the owner's favour.

Liability deserves its own paragraph because its absence is the expensive surprise: Italian apartment living generates neighbour-damage claims as a matter of routine (the upstairs pipe is a national institution), awards track repair costs plus inconvenience, and RC modules price at €30-80 a year inside a package. No rational absentee owner runs a Como apartment without it, and no bank fire policy includes it.

Water-damage statistics explain the Italian premium structure better than any brochure: danni da acqua consistently lead the country's home-claims tables, with the typical apartment claim (a failed flexible hose, a corroded riser, a neighbour's ceiling) settling in the €2,000-8,000 band, high frequency and modest severity, which is why the water module prices meaningfully while whole-package premiums stay low. For an absentee owner the actuarial lesson converts to plumbing: a €150 automatic water-shutoff valve and closed stopcocks between stays cut the modal risk more than any clause, several mainstream insurers openly discount 5-10% for exactly those two measures, and the claim that never happens spares the 1,000 km logistics chain that makes even small, fully covered losses disproportionately annoying to manage from a distance.

Occupancy clauses are the second-home trap inside otherwise friendly wording: theft and water modules commonly restrict cover when the home stands empty beyond 30-90 consecutive days, exactly the pattern of a holiday asset. The fix is contractual, not behavioural: declare the use honestly, buy the second-home variant, and the clause disappears for a 10-20% premium load, the same honesty logic that governs [letting declarations](/guides/italy-holiday-let-licensing/) below.

## How do quake and flood extensions price, and who should buy them?

Catastrophe cover attaches to the multirischi as an extension, priced by hazard: quake at roughly 0.4-1.2 per mille of rebuild value by [seismic zone](/guides/seismic-zones-italy-property/) and construction era, flood similarly banded by basin mapping, both carrying deductibles of 2-10% of the sum insured, and both held by that famous 7.3% of Italian homes.

Worked Como examples anchor the bands:

| Asset | Hazard profile | Extension premium |
|---|---|---|
| Lakefront apartment, rebuild €350,000 | zone 3-4 quake, lake flood band | €250-500/yr combined |
| Hillside villa, rebuild €600,000 | zone 3 quake, slope water risk | €400-900/yr |
| Comparison: Abruzzo stone house | zone 1 quake | €700-1,100/yr quake alone |

Flood pricing rewards the same map-reading discipline as quake: Italy's basin authorities publish hazard maps in 3 bands, insurers rate against them plus your floor level (a first-floor Como apartment prices differently from the ground-floor unit below it), and cellar contents are commonly sub-limited or excluded, worth knowing before storing anything valuable downhill of a lake.

The purchase logic is portfolio logic, stated in 3 lines:

1. **Buy where the hazard is real**: zones 1-2, mapped flood basins, slopes with drainage history; the premium is small against a tail that can equal the asset.
2. **Skip consciously where it is not**: a zone-4 Sardinian apartment reasonably declines the quake line and spends the premium on water and liability instead.
3. **Never confuse the bank's policy with cover**: lender fire policies exclude the catastrophe lines entirely, so «insured through the mortgage» describes the bank's protection, not yours.

The policy-market context strengthens the case for acting while it is unfashionable: the state's 2025 corporate mandate signalled the direction of travel, post-disaster aid for households remains slow and discretionary, and premiums today price a 7.3%-penetration market still competing for volume rather than repricing a mandated one.

## What do letting and condominio change about the insurance file?

Use changes risk and risk must be declared: letting a property converts the policy's assumptions (occupancy, who holds keys, wear patterns), an undeclared commercial use is the classic ground for claim refusal, and the market answers with landlord and host configurations that price the truth at a 10-30% load rather than punishing it at claim time.

The letting owner's checklist, three items long:

1. **Declare the use precisely**: long lets, short lets and mixed patterns are different underwriting answers; the [STR rules](/guides/short-term-rental-rules-italy/) file (CIN, guest registration) will surface in any serious claim anyway.
2. **Add the landlord modules**: tenant-caused damage, loss of rent after an insured event, and liability tuned to paying guests; short-let operators should compare host-specific products against platform guarantees, which are settlements of last resort, not policies.
3. **Reconcile with the building**: the condominio's globale fabbricati covers common structure on terms the amministratore holds; your policy covers your unit's interior, contents and liability, and the boundary between the two (internal fixtures, betterments, your renovation) is where double-cover and no-cover both hide. One email to the amministratore for the building policy's massimali, one broker review against it, and the map is drawn, the same 5-document diligence habit the [condominio guide](/guides/condominio-rules-foreign-owners/) prescribes at purchase.

Guest-facing owners should also mind the documentation loop between licence and policy: insurers ask for the CIN and the declared use at claim time, mismatches between the platform listing, the licence and the policy's declared occupancy are the refusal pattern brokers warn about most, and aligning the three documents costs one afternoon a year.

Sums insured close the file's quality gap: Italian policies apply the proportional rule (underinsure by 30% and claims pay out at 70%), so the number to insure is rebuild cost per sqm (commonly €1,200-1,800 for standard construction, more for historic finishes), never market value, which in Como's lakefront market can run 3-5 times rebuild and would triple premiums for zero extra protection. An annual 15-minute review against renovation spend keeps the ratio honest, and belongs in the same calendar as the [running-costs check](/guides/italy-property-management-costs/).

## How do claims work for a non-resident owner?

Claims run on the policy's clock and the perito's assessment: notification within the contractual window (3-10 days is standard), documentation by photograph and, for theft, a police report (denuncia), then the insurer's loss adjuster values the damage against your sums insured and modules, with payment following acceptance on timelines of weeks for clean files.

Timelines behave when files do: Italian insurers settle documented, uncontested home claims in 30-60 days as a working norm, the perito's visit landing within 1-2 weeks of notification on ordinary losses, and the whole sequence runs on email and the manager's keys without the owner crossing a border.

Distance is manageable with 4 standing arrangements:

- **A local keyholder**: property manager or trusted neighbour who can open, photograph and meet the perito within the notification window.
- **The policy copy accessible**: PDF in your files and your manager's, with the claims number and your broker's contact on page 1.
- **A running inventory**: dated photographs of interiors and valuables, refreshed after renovations; it halves the negotiation with any perito.
- **A broker rather than a call centre**: broker-placed policies come with a human who files, chases and argues; their commission is embedded either way, so use one.

Proportional-rule arithmetic deserves a worked example because it is the payout surprise nobody reads about in advance: insure a home whose rebuild cost is €400,000 for €280,000 (70% of value) and the rule pays every claim at 70%, so a €20,000 water loss settles at €14,000 minus deductible even though the sum insured was 14 times the damage. The rule exists to keep premiums honest, it applies automatically, and it converts every renovation you fund into a silent underinsurance unless the sums move with the spend: €60,000 of new interiors on an unchanged policy is a 13% haircut waiting for its first claim. The annual review's first question is therefore always the same one: what does this house cost to rebuild today, per square metre, finishes included.

The four classic foreign-owner mistakes, collected from claims practice, close the guide because each is cheap to fix and expensive to keep: insuring market value instead of rebuild cost (premium waste), underinsuring renovated interiors (proportional-rule haircuts), skipping liability (the neighbour's ceiling), and treating the bank's fire policy as protection (it protects the bank). One annual broker review, €0 in most relationships, retires all four, and slots the insurance file into the same once-a-year service rhythm as the rest of a well-run [Italian ownership](/guides/buy-property-italy-foreigner/).
