## Abuso edilizio: the check that decides whether the sale happens

**Quick answer:** An abuso edilizio is building work done without the permission it needed, or done differently from the permission granted. It is the most common reason an Italian purchase fails late, and it is invisible to every check a foreign buyer instinctively makes. The register says nothing about it. The notaio does not test for it. It is found by comparing the building to its own permits, and that comparison is a technical job.

The uncomfortable part is how ordinary the cause usually is. Not fraud, and rarely anything anyone regarded as illegal at the time: a terrace glazed in the 1980s, a taverna dug out under a garden, an attic floored and heated, a window moved a metre. Decades later the property is sold to somebody who checks.

## Why it stops a sale rather than merely complicating one

Italian law makes the building titles part of the deed. A deed transferring a building has to identify the permit under which it was built, or the amnesty application under which it was regularised. A deed that cannot do that is void, not merely irregular, and a notaio who suspects the position is unclear will stop rather than sign.

That is why the question cannot be deferred. It arrives at the worst possible moment, with the deposit paid and the buyer committed, unless somebody raised it early. The sequence that avoids this is set out in [due diligence before the compromesso](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/), and the reason the timing is unforgiving is in [the caparra and what the deposit buys you](/guides/caparra-confirmatoria-italy-deposit/).

Three further consequences follow a discovery after completion, and they compound.

The comune can order restoration to the permitted state, and where the work is substantial that means demolition at the owner's expense. Lenders decline: an Italian bank instructs its own valuer, and a property whose compliance cannot be shown is not security. And resale becomes slow, because the next buyer runs exactly the check that found the problem.

## The permission ladder, and what each rung covers

Not all work needs a permit, and knowing the ladder tells you which questions matter.

| Regime | Typical scope | What is required |
|---|---|---|
| Edilizia libera | Maintenance, repainting, replacing fittings like for like | Nothing filed |
| CILA | Ordinary internal works not touching structure | A notice, filed before starting |
| SCIA | Internal restructuring, works affecting the layout | A certified notice from a professional |
| SCIA alternativa / permesso di costruire | New building, substantial restructuring, volume or use changes | An application, examined and granted |

Two points matter more than the categories themselves. The boundaries shift with regional and municipal rules, so a work that needed only a notice in one comune may have needed an application in another. And the regime that applied is the one in force **when the work was done**, not today, which is why dating an alteration is half the analysis.

## Stato legittimo: the document that answers the question

Since the concept was written into the building code, Italian practice has had a single answer to "is this building legal", and it has a name.

The **stato legittimo** of a property is the condition established by the title that permitted its construction, together with the titles for any later works, and it can be certified by a qualified professional. Where the building matches, you have a document. Where it does not, you have a list of what diverges, which is the beginning of a costed conversation rather than the end of the purchase.

Two refinements are worth knowing.

For buildings begun before **1 September 1967**, when the permit obligation was extended nationally, no construction title exists to produce. Legitimacy is established differently, through the documentation that does exist and a sworn declaration, and an older rural property is normally in this position rather than in trouble.

Small deviations do not break legitimacy. The building code sets tolerance thresholds, widened considerably in 2024, inside which a difference between the drawing and the building is not treated as a violation. Where a discrepancy falls inside them, the professional certifies legitimacy notwithstanding. What changed, and by how much, is in [the Salva Casa decree explained](/guides/decreto-salva-casa-italy-explained/).

## Two conformities, and they are not the same check

Foreign buyers, and a surprising number of English-language guides, collapse two separate questions into one.

**Conformità urbanistica ed edilizia** asks whether the building matches its permits. That is the compliance question, and it is answered by comparing the building to the planning file at the comune.

**Conformità catastale** asks whether the building matches its cadastral plan. That is a fiscal and descriptive question, answered at the Agenzia delle Entrate, and the deed carries a declaration about it. A property can be perfectly compliant with its permits and wrong at the catasto, or the reverse.

The second is the cheaper problem and the more commonly mentioned one, which is exactly why it gets confused with the first. What the cadastre can and cannot tell you is set out in [the visura catastale and what it does not prove](/guides/visura-catastale-italy-explained/).

A third document sits alongside them. **Agibilità**, the certificate that the property is fit for use, is filed for a building that meets the hygiene, safety and energy requirements. Older properties frequently have none, which is not automatically a problem, but its absence is a question to ask rather than a detail to skip.

## The amnesties, and the files nobody closed

Italy ran three building amnesties: in **1985**, in **1994** and in **2003**. Each let owners apply to regularise work already carried out, on payment and within limits.

Two facts about them matter to a buyer today.

Where an amnesty application succeeded, the property is regularised and the deed cites the amnesty rather than an original permit. That is a normal and acceptable position.

Where an application was filed and **never decided**, and there are a great many of these, the property sits in limbo. The work is neither authorised nor formally refused. The file exists at the comune, sometimes with the fee part-paid, and it has to be traced and brought to a conclusion. This is one of the most common findings on older southern Italian property and one of the least visible: nothing about the building looks unusual, and the problem is entirely in an archive.

The practical instruction is narrow. If a seller mentions a condono, ask for the application number, the receipt for the payment, and the comune's decision. If the third item does not exist, that is the finding.

## How the check is actually run

The work is done by a **geometra**, an architect or an engineer, and it has a predictable shape.

- **Pull the planning file** at the comune: every title issued for the property, with the approved drawings
- **Pull the cadastral plan** and the visura for each subalterno in the sale
- **Measure the building** and compare it to both sets of drawings
- **Date any divergence**, because the regime in force when the work was done decides how it is treated
- **Classify what is found**: inside tolerance, regularisable, or not
- **Cost the fix**, where there is one, before the buyer is committed

What this costs is a professional fee measured in hundreds of euro on a normal residential property, against a purchase measured in hundreds of thousands. The economics are not close, and the reason buyers skip it anyway is that nobody in the transaction is paid to insist. The agent is paid on completion. What the [geometra actually does](/guides/geometra-role-italy-property/), and how they differ from a British surveyor, is worth reading before you instruct one.

## Rural and coastal property, where the pattern differs

The typical finding changes with the kind of property, and two categories carry their own version of the problem.

On **rural property**, the recurring issue is a building that exists lawfully as something other than a house. Agricultural annexes, stalle, fienili and depositi were built under an agricultural title, and converting one into living space is a change of use requiring its own permission. A stone outbuilding fitted out as a guest room decades ago is a very common finding, and it is a change-of-use question rather than a construction one. Boundaries and rights of way over neighbouring land belong in the same survey, because on farmland they are frequently established by long use rather than by a deed.

On **coastal and scenic property**, the constraint regime dominates. Much of the Italian coastline sits under landscape protection, and inside it works that would be routine elsewhere require a separate authorisation, granted or refused on aesthetic grounds. A terrace enclosed on the Amalfi coast is not the same problem as the same terrace enclosed in a Milanese suburb, and the routes to regularise it are narrower. Where a property is also listed, the position is governed by [heritage restrictions on Italian property](/guides/heritage-restricted-property-italy/) rather than by the ordinary building code.

## The four routes out, and roughly what each costs

A finding is not a verdict. There are four ways a divergence ends, and a surveyor should tell you which one applies before you decide anything.

**Certify it as tolerated.** Where the difference falls inside the statutory bands, no procedure is needed at all: the professional certifies legitimacy and the file closes. Cost is the survey you were paying for anyway.

**Regularise it under the simplified route.** For partial deviations, missing notices and essential variations, the lighter procedure introduced in 2024 applies, with a payment to the comune set by reference to the value the work added. This is now the commonest outcome for old domestic alterations.

**Regularise it under the full route.** Serious breaches still require compliance with both the planning and the building rules, measured at two different moments, and many fail that test. Where they pass, the cost is a professional fee plus the sum the comune assesses.

**Restore or demolish.** Where nothing can be regularised, the work has to be undone. On an added storey or an enclosed volume this is expensive and slow, and it is the outcome that makes a purchase not worth completing.

The ordering matters more than the amounts. A buyer who knows which of the four applies is negotiating; a buyer who knows only that "there is something with the permits" is guessing, and usually guesses in the seller's favour.

## Raising it without losing the property

Foreign buyers often hesitate to ask, on the reasonable theory that a seller in a competitive market will simply move to somebody less demanding. In practice the request is routine and the wording is what makes the difference.

Ask for the documents rather than for reassurance. The planning file, the cadastral plan, the agibilità if one exists, and, where the seller has one, a professional's certification of stato legittimo. A seller who has sold an Italian property before expects this list.

Then put it in the contract. Make the preliminary conditional on the certification being produced and clean, with the deposit returnable if it is not. This is ordinary Italian practice and it moves the risk to the party who can actually resolve it. Which deposit wording gives you that protection is not a detail: the difference between the three types is set out in [the caparra and what the deposit buys](/guides/caparra-confirmatoria-italy-deposit/).

Where a seller resists both requests, treat the resistance as the finding. It is cheaper information than a survey.

## Weighing an imperfect property

Very few older Italian properties are perfect on paper, and treating any divergence as fatal will cost you the market.

**Positions that are usually workable**

- Deviations inside the statutory tolerances, certified as such
- Cadastral plans that no longer match, updatable at modest cost
- Missing agibilità on an older building otherwise built to its permits
- Minor internal changes falling under the simplified regularisation route
- A completed amnesty, properly documented

**Positions to treat as serious**

- An amnesty application filed and never decided
- Added volume or an added storey with no title
- Anything at all in an area under landscape or heritage constraint, where a separate authorisation applies and the ordinary routes narrow sharply
- Work the seller cannot date, on a property whose file is incomplete
- A seller who declines to let a surveyor into the comune's records

The proportionate approach is to price the finding rather than fear the category. A costed, regularisable divergence is a negotiating position. An uncosted one is an open-ended liability, and the difference between them is a survey. Where the work forms part of a renovation you were planning anyway, [what an Italian renovation costs](/guides/italy-property-renovation-costs-guide/) covers the forward-looking half, and the deed mechanics are in [what signing the rogito achieves](/guides/rogito-italian-deed-of-sale/).
