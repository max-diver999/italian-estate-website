Quick answer: the agibilità is the habitability attestation of an Italian building, since 2016 a professional's certified declaration (SCA) filed within 15 days of completing relevant works, and its absence is one of the commonest findings in resale due diligence: sales without it are valid, but the gap prices at €5,000-15,000 in fix costs and friction, banks lean on it, and case law lets buyers claim where habitability was promised. The buyer's job is classification: never-existed on old stock (curable, €1,000-3,000), missing-after-works (a red flag with a date), or physically unreachable (a walk-away). This guide covers what the document is, the 2016 reform, the check, the cure and the negotiation, inside the wider [due diligence](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/) sequence.

## What does the agibilità actually certify?

The agibilità attests that a building meets the requirements of safety, hygiene, health, energy saving and plant conformity for its declared use, and it costs €0 to ask about and €1,000-3,000 to reconstruct: not the walls-match-plans question (cadastral conformity) and not the energy grade (the APE), but lawful fitness to live in, the third leg of the documentary tripod.

| Document | Certifies | Who produces it |
|---|---|---|
| Agibilità / SCA | lawful habitability of the use | licensed professional, filed at the comune |
| Cadastral conformity | plans match reality | [geometra](/guides/geometra-role-italy-property/)'s comparison |
| APE | energy performance class | accredited certifier |

Stock arithmetic explains why this document generates so many purchase conversations: Italy's median home predates 1975, the habitability paper trail only became systematic in the post-war decades, and practitioner experience consistently puts urban resales with an immediately producible habitability document well below 50%, with the south and the rural markets running lower still. That prevalence is exactly why the market prices the gap mildly on old stock (€5,000-15,000 including friction) instead of treating it as a defect: a missing 1950s abitabilità describes the filing habits of 1955, not the state of the building. The pricing turns hostile only where the gap is modern and specific, which is exactly why the generational table below, and never the bare yes/no answer, is the diagnostic instrument that actually matters.

The content behind the attestation is a bundle of underlying certificates, and knowing the bundle explains both the cost of curing and the ways the document goes missing:

1. **Plant declarations (DiCo)**: electrical and thermal systems certified conformant by the installers.
2. **Static/structural attestations** where works touched structure, tied to the collaudo chain.
3. **Energy documentation** aligning with the filed APE.
4. **Hygiene and height standards**: minimum room sizes, ceiling heights, ventilation ratios by regulation, the same standards the [Salva Casa](/guides/salva-casa-decree-property-buyers/) mini-flat rules adjusted at the small end.

## How did the 2016 SCA reform change the document?

Decree 222/2016 changed the procedure without touching the substance: the comune-issued certificato di agibilità became the SCA, a certified declaration by a licensed professional filed within 15 days of completing the relevant works, effective immediately and subject to spot checks, ending the era when habitability waited months on a municipal office.

The 15-day filing window is the reform's teeth for buyers: it converts «was an SCA due?» into a date arithmetic anyone can run from the works' completion certificate, and it makes the professional who missed the window findable by name.

The document's generations, as a buyer meets them in files:

| Era | Document | What its absence means |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-1934 buildings | often nothing ever existed | normal; cure by today's SCA if needed |
| 1934-2016 | licenza / certificato di abitabilità-agibilità | may exist in archives; retrieval beats re-certification |
| Post-2016 works | SCA within 15 days of completion | absence after relevant works = specific red flag |
| New builds | SCA in the handover file | pairs with the [warranty documents](/guides/italy-new-build-warranty-defects/) |

The generational reading is the diagnostic skill: a Naples 1950s apartment whose file holds no habitability paper is historically ordinary, and the archive search (the comune's records answer in 2-8 weeks) often surfaces a 1957 abitabilità nobody remembered. The same file on a unit renovated in 2021 tells a different story, because the renovation's completion obliged an SCA filing within 15 days, and its absence dates a specific professional's specific omission, with the works' whole paper trail now worth the [conformity check](/guides/abusi-edilizi-buying-property-italy/)'s closest reading.

Regional practice adds one wrinkle worth knowing: several regions and large comuni digitised their building archives in the 2010s, Naples among the partial cases, so retrieval times swing from days (digital) to 2 months (paper basements), and the geometra quoting your search will know which regime the target comune runs before quoting the timeline.

**Insider tip:** ask the seller's side one two-part question early: «esiste l'agibilità, e di che anno?». The year is the information: a 1965 answer starts an archive retrieval, a «filed with the 2021 works» answer requests a copy, and a shrug on recently renovated stock reprices the file before you have spent a euro on surveys.

## What happens when you buy without agibilità?

The sale is valid and the consequences are financial: Italian courts treat missing habitability as a quality defect rather than a nullity, letting buyers claim damages or price reduction where the state was concealed or promised, while banks discount appraisals or condition loans, and the market prices the gap at €5,000-15,000 on ordinary conforming stock.

Naples supplies the textbook distribution: vast mid-century stock whose papers live in archives if anywhere, a renovation wave since 2015 that obliges modern SCAs, and price bands where a €10,000 friction gap is 4-6% of many deals, wide enough to fund every check this guide lists twice over.

Where the absence actually bites, ranked by frequency:

- **Financing friction**: several lenders want the document or its cure plan; the [mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-italy/)'s approval-rate realities tighten further on gapped files.
- **Resale inheritance**: the next buyer's surveyor asks the question you should ask now; unpriced gaps travel with the deed until someone pays the cure.
- **Letting exposure**: licensing, platform registrations and insurance claims increasingly reference the habitability trail; an undeclared gap surfaces at the worst procedural moments.
- **Damages litigation**: promised-but-missing habitability supports price-reduction claims for years after closing, the case-law line sellers' lawyers know well enough to negotiate around.

Case-law contours give the negotiation its edges: the Cassazione's consistent line treats delivery of a habitable property as part of the seller's core obligation where habitability was promised or implied by the sale's nature, letting buyers pursue price reduction or damages years after closing, with awards tracking the €1,000-3,000 cure cost plus 100% of documented consequential losses. Sellers' lawyers know the line, which is why written as-is clauses («il compratore dichiara di conoscere lo stato...») appear in gapped files: they narrow the buyer's later claims and are, read in reverse, an admission the gap exists. A buyer signing such a clause should have priced the cure already, because the clause's whole purpose is to make the price the final word on the subject, in both directions.

The negotiation mechanics follow the classification. Curable gaps convert to numbers: the professional's quote (€1,000-3,000 on conforming stock) plus a friction premium comes off the price, or the seller cures before the rogito under a condition in the [offer](/guides/italy-property-offer-negotiation/), with a holdback at the notary as the standard enforcement. Structural gaps convert to the [abusi ladder](/guides/abusi-edilizi-buying-property-italy/)'s arithmetic instead, because the missing document is then a symptom, and buying symptoms without pricing diseases is the one pattern this site's whole legal cluster exists to prevent.

## How is a missing agibilità cured, step by step?

The cure on substantially conforming stock is a professional's project of €1,000-3,000 and 4-12 weeks: verify the underlying requirements, assemble or reconstruct the certificates (plants, energy, statics where relevant), remedy small gaps, and file the SCA, a sequence the seller can run before closing or the buyer can price and run after.

| Step | Actor | Cost band | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Archive search for historic documents | geometra at the comune | €100-300 | 2-8 weeks |
| Requirements survey | geometra / perito | €300-600 | 1-2 weeks |
| Plant certifications (missing DiCo) | licensed installers | €200-800 per plant | 1-4 weeks |
| Small remedial works | trades | as quoted | varies |
| SCA drafting and filing | professional | €400-900 | days |

The professional cast is the familiar one: the geometra runs the search and the survey, licensed installers certify or re-certify the plants, and the same professional who signs your SCA carries insurance behind the signature, the accountability design the 2016 reform traded for the comune's slow stamp.

Sequencing decides who funds it, and the market's standard answers are stable: on listed stock the [seller's preparation](/guides/selling-property-italy-foreigner/) ideally closes the gap before the first viewing, because cured files sell at clean-file prices; under offer, the conformity condition puts the cure on the seller's account with the rogito as deadline; bought as-is, the buyer prices cure plus friction and enjoys the discount. The only wrong sequence is the silent one, where nobody prices the gap and the next transaction discovers it with interest.

Archive economics favour retrieval before reconstruction in every mid-century file: the comune's building archive answers document requests for €100-300 in fees, roughly half of the «missing» habitability papers of the 1950s-1980s turn out to exist quietly in those municipal drawers, and a retrieved 1962 certificate cures the file for 10% of a re-certification's cost. The 2-8 week response times are the constraint, which is why the request belongs in the same first week as the visure, not after the offer: an archive answer arriving mid-negotiation is pure leverage, while the same answer arriving after the rogito is expensive trivia. Sellers can run the identical search before listing for the identical fee, and the ones who do convert a buyer objection into a folder tab.

Rural and historic edge cases deserve their own expectations: farmhouses converted across generations may need genuine works (heights, ventilation, stairs) before any professional can certify, and the cure quote then belongs in the renovation budget rather than the paperwork line, with the [renovation guide](/guides/italy-property-renovation-costs-guide/)'s per-sqm bands doing the pricing. That is not a reason to avoid such stock: it is the reason such stock costs what it costs, and the buyer who knows which €2,000 problems are wearing €20,000 disguises, and vice versa, is the buyer this documentary tripod was always meant to serve.

## How does agibilità fit the buyer's overall document check?

Costs across this guide compress into one planning line: €0 to ask, €100-300 to search the archive, €300-600 to survey, €1,000-3,000 to cure conforming stock, and €5,000-15,000 of market friction avoided when any of those numbers lands before the offer instead of after the deed.

The habitability question slots into the standard sequence as one line with three possible answers: present (verify year and scope), absent-but-curable (price the €1,000-3,000 and assign it), or absent-and-symptomatic (escalate to the full conformity investigation), and the routing decision costs one question to the seller plus one geometra hour.

One number keeps the whole topic proportionate: across the 4 scenarios below, the habitability line item resolves for under €3,000 in the overwhelming majority of files, and the expensive minority announces itself early through the same 3 signals every time (post-2016 works without an SCA, archive refusals, physical standards the rooms cannot meet).

Buyer scenarios distribute the attention correctly across the market's stock:

1. **Post-2016 renovated urban stock**: the SCA should exist by law; request the copy, verify the filing date against the works' completion, escalate on absence.
2. **Mid-century city apartments (the Naples norm)**: expect historic documents or archival silence; budget the retrieval, price the cure if silence, and treat neither as alarming.
3. **New builds**: the SCA arrives in the handover file beside the decennale; its absence at consegna is a developer conversation, not a buyer's cost.
4. **Rural conversions**: assume the certification question is a works question; survey first, romance second.

The document's deeper function, like everything in this cluster, is sequencing leverage: every item on the tripod (conformity, energy, habitability) is cheap to verify before the offer and expensive to litigate after the deed, and the whole verification stack, geometra, archives, quotes, runs €500-1,500 against purchase prices three orders of magnitude larger. Italian property rewards buyers who read paper in the same proportion that it punishes buyers who read only light and stone, and the agibilità, the least glamorous document in the file, is where that rule collects most of its quiet victories.
