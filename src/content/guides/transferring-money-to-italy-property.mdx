Quick answer: paying for an Italian property means moving cleared, documented funds into the eurozone ahead of two fixed moments, the compromesso deposit and the rogito balance, then paying by assegno circolare or irrevocable transfer that the deed itself records. Cash stops at €5,000, three different gatekeepers run anti-money-laundering checks, and the FX route you choose swings the bill by up to 3% of the price. This guide sequences the whole money side: rails, timing, the banker's draft, the notary's escrow option, and source-of-funds files, with the contract mechanics themselves covered in the [compromesso guide](/guides/compromesso-italy-property-contract/).

## How does money legally move in an Italian property purchase?

Italian law makes the payment trail part of the title itself: since 2007 every deed must declare how the price was paid, instrument by instrument with dates and amounts, and cash participation stops at €5,000 under the current cap. The practical consequence is a purchase funded only through banks, planned around two settlement moments.

The canonical flow, from foreign account to keys:

1. **Fund** an Italian or EU account in your name by SEPA or SWIFT.
2. **Pay the caparra** at compromesso, typically 10-20% of price, by transfer or draft.
3. **Issue the assegno circolare** (or arrange the irrevocable transfer) days before the rogito.
4. **Settle at the deed**: instruments hand over against signature; the notary records each one.
5. **Declare**: the deed's payment-means clause lists everything, creating a permanent public record.

| Rule | Source | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Payment traceability in the deed | L. 40/2007 | every instrument declared |
| Cash cap | €5,000 (L. 197/2022) | applies to any part, deposits included |
| Notary price escrow | L. 124/2017 c. 63-67 | on request, either party |
| AML verification | D.Lgs. 231/2007 | bank, notary, agent all check |

## Which transfer rail should you use: SEPA, SWIFT or broker?

Rail choice is a cost decision first and a timing decision second: SEPA moves euros inside the zone in one day at trivial cost, SWIFT moves any currency in 1-5 days at €20-60 per message plus intermediary fees, and the FX conversion itself, wherever it happens, carries the real price tag of 0.3-4% depending on provider.

| Route | Timing | Explicit cost | Hidden FX margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEPA (EUR to EUR) | same/next day | €0-15 | none |
| SWIFT via your bank | 1-5 days | €20-60 + intermediaries | 2-4% typical retail |
| FX broker / multi-currency fintech | 1-3 days | usually €0 | 0.3-1% |
| Forward contract (broker) | locks 30-180 days ahead | in the rate | 0.4-1.2% all-in |

Sequencing rules that save real money:

- Convert once, not twice: send the home currency to the broker, convert to euros there, SEPA the euros onward; double conversions through correspondent banks stack margins.
- Season the funds: money should sit in your named account before it moves to Italy, because same-week hops between three jurisdictions are what AML reviews flag.
- Test the pipe with €500 before the six-figure tranche; a mistyped IBAN found early costs nothing.

**Insider tip:** ask your Italian bank in writing how many days it needs between an incoming SWIFT credit and issuing an assegno circolare against it. Answers range from zero to 5 working days by bank and branch, and that answer, not the flight schedule, sets your real rogito date.

## What is the assegno circolare and how do you get one?

An assegno circolare is a banker's draft an Italian bank issues only after debiting your account for 100% of its face value, which makes it cleared money in paper form: the seller at the rogito exchanges title against value that cannot bounce. Issuance costs €2-10 per draft, and it remains the default closing instrument nationwide, with irrevocable transfers gaining ground in remote closings.

Obtaining one is procedural, not discretionary, and runs on this checklist:

1. Hold the cleared balance at an Italian bank (or an EU bank with Italian operations) in your name.
2. Order the draft 2-5 working days before signing, made out exactly as the notary's closing note instructs, one draft per payee where the deed splits payments.
3. Collect in branch with ID; some banks courier to the notary directly.
4. Keep the issue receipts: the deed cites each draft's number, bank, and amount.

Draft mechanics explain the instrument's endurance in a SEPA world: an assegno circolare is covered at issuance, the bank blocks or debits the €300,000 before printing the paper, is payable only to the named payee, non-transferable by default above €1,000, and prescribes after 3 years if never cashed, with funds recoverable by the payer. Fraud rates are negligible next to transfer-interception scams, which is why sellers' lawyers still ask for paper on six-figure closings: a doctored IBAN in a hacked email chain has diverted many a balance, while a draft handed across the table against the deed's signature settles 100% visibly. The one operational risk is logistical, a draft made out with a misspelled payee needs reissuing by the same branch, so the notary's closing note spelling governs, letter for letter.

Where attending in person is impossible, the remote-closing toolkit, powers of attorney, the notary's own account as conduit, and irrevocable SEPA instructions with value dates, is covered in the [remote purchase guide](/guides/how-to-buy-italy-property-remotely/), and it changes the instruments without changing the traceability rules.

## Should you use the notary's price deposit (deposito prezzo)?

The deposito prezzo converts Italian closing practice into genuine escrow at a cost of €0-300: on request by either party, the buyer pays 100% of the balance into the notary's segregated account, released to the seller only after registration confirms no adverse formality, a prior transcription or an undisclosed mortgage, surfaced in the gap. Since 2017 this is a statutory right, not a favour.

When it earns its place:

| Situation | Why the deposit helps |
|---|---|
| Seller's mortgage to be cancelled from proceeds | notary pays the bank first, seller gets the net |
| Any hint of creditor exposure on the seller | funds move only against a clean post-registration check |
| Remote closing with POA | your money never sits with a counterparty |
| New-build completion with snags open | leverage survives the signature |

The notary's segregated account is insolvency-remote by law, interest accrues to a public fund rather than to either party, and the typical incremental cost is zero to a few hundred euros inside the notarial bill. The notary's broader gatekeeping role, and why Italian closings concentrate so much verification in one officer, is profiled in the [notaio guide](/guides/notaio-italy-property-role/).

Registration timelines make the mechanism concrete, and they are short by design: notaries must register deeds within 30 days and in urban practice file most within 2-5 working days, so the seller's wait for released funds is measured in days, not months. The protected window matters because Italian priority runs on transcription order: a creditor's attachment or a second deed transcribed between your signature and your registration would otherwise take priority over an already-paid buyer, the classic gap risk of civil-law closings. The deposito prezzo re-sequences the risk: money moves only after the registers confirm your deed landed first and clean. For a buyer wiring €400,000 toward a seller they met twice, that re-sequencing is the difference between trusting a stranger and trusting the land registry, at a cost of €0-300 inside the notarial bill.

The seller's side rarely objects: the wait is days, and refusal reads as a signal worth probing.

## What will AML checks actually ask of you?

Anti-money-laundering rules put 3 independent gatekeepers on your money: the bank executing transfers, the notary at the deed, and the estate agent from the first offer, each obliged under the 2007 decree to identify you, trace any beneficial owner above the 25% threshold, and assess the funds' origin. The practical currency of all 3 checks is documents, and the file is assembled once.

The source-of-funds dossier that answers every gatekeeper:

1. **Identity layer**: passport, codice fiscale (the how-to is in the [codice fiscale guide](/guides/codice-fiscale-italy-property/)), proof of address.
2. **Origin layer**: whichever applies: completion statement of a property sold, 12 months of savings statements, an executed gift deed, a company dividend resolution, an inheritance grant.
3. **Path layer**: statements showing the money moving from origin account to the account that pays Italy, without unexplained intermediate stops.

Beneficial-ownership rules reach through every wrapper a buyer might use: a purchase by an LLC, trust or family company obliges the notary to identify the natural persons owning above 25% or otherwise controlling the vehicle, and to verify them as if they were the buyers, passports, tax codes and source of funds included. Screening also covers politically exposed persons, who face enhanced checks rather than refusal, and sanctions lists, which stop a closing outright. For the ordinary private buyer the practical footprint is 3 document sets and 15 minutes of honest questions; for structured purchases it is a genuine, document-heavy workstream to start 60-90 days ahead, because a notary who cannot complete verification must refuse the deed, and refusal on closing week forfeits calendars, drafts and sometimes caparre.

Time kills friction here: documents assembled before the first transfer clear reviews in days, while reconstructing a paper trail after a bank freezes a six-figure credit takes weeks and has sunk closings scheduled around it. The wider pattern, verification early beating remediation late, is the same one the [buyer-mistakes guide](/guides/mistakes-foreign-buyers-italy/) documents across the whole transaction.

**Insider tip:** if any part of the price is a family gift, formalise it before the money moves: a dated gift letter or notarised deed naming donor, amount and purpose. Undocumented family transfers are the single most common AML stall for foreign buyers, and they are trivially preventable a month in advance.

## How do you manage exchange-rate risk between offer and closing?

Currency risk is the largest unmanaged number in most non-euro purchases: between compromesso and rogito sit 60-120 days, and 90-day swings on sterling and dollar pairs regularly exceed 3-4% of the price, dwarfing every negotiable fee in the closing stack. Three instruments cover the exposure, in ascending order of commitment.

| Instrument | Mechanics | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Spot conversion now | convert full amount at today's rate, hold euros | funds already free; simplest |
| Forward contract | lock today's rate for settlement at closing window | funds arriving later (sale proceeds, bonus) |
| Layered tranches | convert thirds at intervals | no strong rate view, moderate risk appetite |

A Rome worked case sets the scale: a £350,000 funding requirement at 1.17 becomes €409,500, but the same pounds at 1.12 a quarter later produce €392,000, a €17,500 hole that exceeds the entire notary-plus-registration bill on a typical second home from the [closing-costs breakdown](/guides/italy-property-closing-costs-breakdown/). A forward at 1.165 would have fixed €407,750 on day one, spending €1,750 of rate to delete €17,500 of uncertainty.

Discipline notes: hedge the committed amount only, never speculate past the caparra you would genuinely forfeit; use authorised, safeguarded providers; and diarise the forward's value date against the rogito, rolling once, early, if the notary's calendar slips, rather than in the final week when rolls price worst.

Funding scenarios stress different parts of the pipeline. The cash buyer from sterling or dollars carries FX risk as the dominant line and should price forwards first. The sale-proceeds buyer, funding from a home-country completion, carries timing risk instead: their caparra must not outrun their own buyer's contract. The mortgage-assisted buyer splits the flow, deposit from savings now, balance from the bank at rogito, and needs the two streams reconciled in the notary's closing note a week ahead. Naming your scenario early lets every adviser sequence around its weakest link.

## What does the full payment timeline look like?

A funded, hedged, documented purchase runs on a timeline where money always arrives one step before the legal act that spends it, and the whole sequence spans 90-120 days on typical resale deals. Compression is possible, cash-rich buyers close in 30 days, but each compressed step removes slack the banks may claim back.

| Week | Money action | Legal act it precedes |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | AML dossier assembled; codice fiscale obtained | offer |
| 1-2 | test transfer; caparra funds positioned in EU | proposta accepted |
| 2-4 | caparra paid by transfer/draft (10-20%) | compromesso signed |
| 3-6 | forward contract locked for balance | — |
| 10-14 | balance lands in Italian account; seasoning | — |
| 15-16 | assegni circolari issued per closing note | rogito |
| 16 | instruments settle; deed declares them; deposito prezzo releases | registration |

The list-form rules that keep the table honest: never schedule the rogito closer than 5 working days after the balance's expected landing; never let the caparra travel on the same day the compromesso signs; and reconcile the notary's closing note against the [full cost stack](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/) a week ahead, because late surprises in Italian closings are almost always cost lines nobody had funded, not legal defects nobody had found. Eligibility, contracts and the rest of the legal spine live in the [foreign buyer hub](/guides/buy-property-italy-foreigner/).
