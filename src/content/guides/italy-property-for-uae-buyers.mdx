**Quick answer:** Italy asks one question about a non-EU buyer and it is a question about citizenship. The notary reads the Foreign Ministry reciprocity table for the country on your passport, not for the country on your residence visa, which catches Gulf-based buyers more than anyone else. Separately, because the UAE levies no personal income tax, whatever Italy takes is final and there is no credit anywhere to soften it.

<TldrBlock text="Reciprocity is tested on citizenship, art. 16 preleggi · the Emirates ID is not the document being checked · dual nationals may rely on either passport · an Italian residence permit removes the test · no UAE income tax means no credit, so the Italian election is final"/>

A buyer in Dubai typically holds a passport from one country, a residence visa from another, banks in a third and is buying in a fourth. Italian property law cuts through that with a single question, and it is not the one most Gulf-based buyers expect.

## Which document is the notary actually looking at?

Article 16 of the preliminary provisions to the Italian Civil Code makes a foreign national's civil capacity conditional on reciprocity: whether their own state would allow an Italian to acquire property there on comparable terms. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs publishes tables country by country and the notary consults them before the deed.

The connecting factor is nationality. Where you live, where you pay tax and where your salary is credited are all irrelevant to this test. A residence visa establishes your right to be in the Emirates; it says nothing about whether Italy grants you capacity to buy.

That produces outcomes that feel wrong to people who have built their lives in the Gulf. An Indian national with 15 years in Dubai, an Emirates ID, a Dubai mortgage history and a Dubai bank is assessed against India. A Pakistani national in the same position is assessed against Pakistan. Two colleagues in the same office with the same residence status can receive different answers.

- The check happens weeks before the deed, as part of the notary's file
- A failure means the buyer lacks capacity and the deed would be void, so it is not a formality to hurry
- Table entries can be partial, permitting urban residential property while restricting agricultural land
- Our [reciprocity guide](/guides/italy-reciprocity-property-foreigners/) sets out how the entries read and where the qualifications bite

## What are the two routes around an unfavourable entry?

Both are legitimate and both are used routinely, which is worth saying because the reciprocity question is often presented as a dead end.

The first is a second nationality. Where a buyer holds two passports, capacity can rest on whichever one satisfies the table. This is common enough in the Gulf that it is worth raising with the notary at the first document request rather than after an unfavourable answer, since the file is simply built on the other nationality from the start.

The second is Italian residence. A non-EU national holding an Italian residence permit for work, family reasons, study or long-term residence falls outside the reciprocity requirement, because residence in Italy answers the question that reciprocity was asking. That route is slower and has consequences of its own, since Italian residence brings Italian worldwide taxation, but it removes the capacity question permanently.

| Buyer | What decides capacity |
|---|---|
| Emirati national | Reciprocity table for the United Arab Emirates |
| Indian national resident in Dubai | Reciprocity table for India |
| Dual national | Whichever nationality satisfies the table |
| Any non-EU national with an Italian permit | The permit, not the table |

- **Insider tip:** disclose all nationalities to the notary at the outset, since building the file twice costs weeks
- A company purchase is assessed on the place of incorporation rather than on the shareholders' nationalities
- Where the answer is partial, the classification of every parcel in the sale matters, not just the building

## Why does no UAE income tax make the Italian choice harder, not easier?

It removes the safety net. In most of this cluster the buyer's home country taxes the Italian rent and grants a credit for the Italian tax, so an inefficient Italian election is partly corrected at home. With no personal income tax in the UAE there is no home-country charge, no credit, and no correction.

Italian tax is therefore the whole tax and the Italian election is the whole decision. Under the flat cedolare secca regime a short let is charged at 21% on the first residential unit and 26% on the second and beyond; under ordinary IRPEF the tax is progressive but expenses are deductible. Our [cedolare secca guide](/guides/cedolare-secca-italy-rental-property/) sets out the comparison, and for a UAE-resident owner the calculation has only one column.

That has a clear practical consequence. A property with heavy running costs, a villa with a pool, a garden and substantial condominio charges, can favour the ordinary route despite its higher headline rates, and no adviser in Dubai will catch that error because there is no Emirati return on which it would show up.

- Model the Italian regimes on the actual property costs, not on the headline rates
- The election is made per property and per year, so a portfolio can mix regimes
- IMU is an annual cost on a second home and is not deductible under the flat regime

## Which third system might still reach you?

This is the question a Gulf address makes easiest to overlook, and it is the one most likely to produce an unpleasant surprise.

Residence in a no-tax jurisdiction does not neutralise every other system. Some states tax their citizens wherever they live, so a United States citizen in Dubai carries full US obligations on an Italian property regardless of the Emirates. Some tax systems continue to reach recent emigrants for a defined period, so a buyer who left their home country in the last few years may still be within its charge. And some countries assess on domicile or on family connections rather than purely on days present.

The decision framework is to establish three positions before buying rather than two: what Italy takes, what the UAE takes, and what the passport country takes. Most Gulf-based buyers do the first two and stop.

- **Red flag:** advice that treats a UAE residence as the end of the tax analysis
- Recent emigrants should confirm whether a trailing residence rule still applies to them
- Citizenship-based taxation is rare but absolute where it applies

## What is different about the practical mechanics?

Two things, both procedural.

Money movement is documented more heavily. The purchase balance travels through traceable channels and the notary records the payment details in the deed, and the source of funds has to satisfy anti-money-laundering requirements. A transfer from a Gulf account will usually attract more questions than an intra-EU one, and the answer is preparation rather than argument: salary records, sale contracts for whatever funded the purchase, and consistent documentation. Our [money transfer guide](/guides/transferring-money-to-italy-property/) covers the mechanics and the currency question.

Time at the property is governed by the passport as well. UAE nationals have visa-free access to the Schengen area for short stays, which means the 90 days in any 180-day period covered in our [stay-limit guide](/guides/90-180-day-rule-italy-property-owners/). Holders of other passports follow their own state's position, which for some nationalities means applying for a visa for each visit. The Entry/Exit System automates the counting from 2026.

## Does a UAE company change the picture?

It changes it in three places, and since 2023 the answer is different from what long-established Gulf practice assumes.

The United Arab Emirates introduced a federal corporate tax at 9% on business profits above the statutory threshold, effective for financial years beginning on or after 1 June 2023. Holding structures that were genuinely costless before now sit inside a corporate tax regime, and whether a particular holding company falls within the charge or within an exemption depends on its activity and its qualifying status rather than on its address.

On the Italian side, capacity is assessed differently for a company. Reciprocity for legal persons turns on the place of incorporation, which for a UAE entity means the table entry for the United Arab Emirates rather than the shareholders' passports. That can be an advantage where an individual shareholder's own nationality would be the harder question, and it is a longer road with its own costs.

The third place is Italian tax on the property itself, which changes shape entirely when the owner is a company rather than an individual. The flat cedolare secca regime is for individuals, so a corporate owner is outside it, and the disposal treatment differs as well: the 5 year exemption in article 67 TUIR is a rule for private sellers and does not apply to a company.

| Owner | Italian rental regime | Gain after 5 years | Capacity assessed on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual | Cedolare secca or IRPEF | Exempt under art. 67 | Citizenship |
| UAE company | Corporate rules, no flat regime | Not exempt | Place of incorporation |

- Structuring for capacity reasons can cost more in Italian tax than the capacity problem was worth
- The 9% UAE charge is recent enough that older advice on Gulf holding structures predates it
- Where a structure already exists for other reasons, price the Italian consequences before adding the property to it

## What does the dirham peg do to the purchase?

It converts a euro purchase into a dollar decision, which is easy to miss from a jurisdiction that thinks of its currency as stable.

The dirham has been pegged to the United States dollar at a fixed rate for decades, so a buyer converting AED into EUR is effectively converting USD into EUR at whatever the market offers on the day. The stability a Gulf-based buyer experiences day to day is stability against the dollar, not against the euro, and the euro-dollar rate has moved by more than 10% within single years on several occasions.

That matters at three moments: the deposit, the balance at the deed, and any later transfer to fund works. A purchase agreed in euro months before completion carries a real and unhedged exposure in the interval, and the exposure is larger than most buyers assume precisely because they are used to a fixed rate.

- Fix the euro cost early where the timetable allows, rather than converting at the deed
- Staged payments on an off-plan purchase spread the exposure across the construction period
- The peg means an Italian mortgage is a euro liability against a dollar-linked income, which is a currency position rather than a hedge

## Which buyer scenarios does this reshape?

**The Emirati national buying a European base.** The straightforward case. Reciprocity resolved on the UAE entry, visa-free short stays, no home-country tax on the rent, Italian tax final. This profile has fewer moving parts than almost any other buyer in this cluster.

**The long-term expatriate in Dubai.** The profile this page exists for. The reciprocity answer comes from a country they may not have lived in for decades, and the tax analysis needs a third leg. Both are solvable and neither is discovered by assuming Dubai is the answer.

**The buyer intending to relocate to Italy.** The Italian residence route removes the reciprocity question and replaces it with Italian worldwide taxation, which for someone accustomed to a no-tax jurisdiction is a substantial change rather than an administrative one. Model it before committing.

**The investor letting the property from abroad.** Italian licensing applies in full, the CIN and the comune's rules do not care where the owner lives, and remote management costs should be in the model from the start rather than added later.
