Quick answer: judicial auctions are the one Italian channel where the state, not a seller, prices the property, and prices fall roughly 25% with every failed round. A foreign bidder needs only a codice fiscale, a 10% deposit and the discipline to pay the balance within the deadline, typically 120 days. In exchange for the discount you give up seller warranties, agency hand-holding and, sometimes, vacant possession on day one. This guide walks the full route: finding lots on the public portal, reading the perizia, bidding, taxes by court decree, and the occupied-property scenarios that decide whether a bargain was real. The conventional purchase route is covered in the [step-by-step buying guide](/guides/how-to-buy-italy-property-step-by-step/) for comparison.

## How do Italian property auctions work?

An asta giudiziaria is a court-ordered sale of foreclosed or bankruptcy property, run by a judge's delegate at a base price set by an independent appraisal, with offers admissible down to 25% below that base. No estate agent stands between bidder and court: the file, the rules and the calendar are public, and the process is identical for Italians and foreigners.

The mechanism compresses into five fixed stages:

1. **Publication**: every lot must appear on the national portal at least 45 days before the hearing.
2. **Offer filing**: sealed or telematic offer with a cauzione of 10% of your bid.
3. **Hearing**: one valid offer wins at that price; several trigger a raise-off (gara) in fixed increments.
4. **Balance**: saldo prezzo within the notice deadline, 120 days at most.
5. **Decree**: the judge signs the decreto di trasferimento, which transfers title and cancels the debtor's mortgages.

| Stage | Who acts | Typical timing |
|---|---|---|
| Publication on the portal | Court / delegate | 45+ days before hearing |
| Offer + 10% deposit | Bidder | Up to 1 business day before |
| Hearing and gara | Delegate | Day zero |
| Saldo prezzo | Winner | 60-120 days |
| Transfer decree | Judge | 2-8 weeks after balance |

## Where do you find auction properties in Italy?

Every judicial sale in the country is published on the portale delle vendite pubbliche, the justice ministry's portal, at least 45 days before the hearing, and the listing carries the three documents that matter: the appraisal (perizia), the sale notice (avviso di vendita) and the photographs. Commercial aggregators repackage the same feed with better search filters.

Reading a lot page in the right order saves wasted viewings:

1. Open the **avviso di vendita** first: base price, minimum admissible offer, deposit rules, balance deadline, hearing date.
2. Then the **perizia**: cadastral identity, building irregularities, occupancy status, condominium arrears, and the appraiser's value.
3. Only then the photos, which are often old and always flattering to nobody.

Portale delle vendite pubbliche listings are legally constitutive: a sale not published there at least 45 days ahead is voidable, which is why the portal is complete in a way no commercial site can promise. Each lot carries an RGE case number, the court, the delegate's contacts, and the three documents that decide a bid, with visit bookings handled through the same page. Coverage runs to tens of thousands of active lots nationally at any moment, from €30,000 rural ruins to €2 million villas in receivership, and the feed updates as hearings conclude. Aggregators add map search and email alerts on top of the identical data, so the sensible workflow is alerts on an aggregator, then verification of every document on the official page, dates and case number included, before any money moves anywhere.

The perizia is a court-commissioned survey you get free, something no private listing gives you, but it is not a substitute for your own checks: appraisals can predate the hearing by 2 years or more. The verification habits from the [due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/) apply with more force here, not less, because auction sales carry no seller warranties at all.

**Insider tip:** courts publish visit slots managed by the custodian, and requests go through the portal, not through any agent. A lot you cannot get inside before the hearing is a lot you are pricing blind; treat a refused or impossible visit as a red flag worth more than any discount.

## How is the auction price set, and how deep do discounts go?

The base price is the court appraisal at the first attempt, and each deserted auction authorises a reduction of up to 25% for the next round, while bidders may already offer 25% below base (offerta minima) at any round. Stock that reaches a third attempt therefore sits 40-55% below its original appraisal, which is where the famous bargains and the famous mistakes both live.

Worked descent on a Bologna apartment appraised at €280,000:

| Round | Base price | Minimum admissible offer |
|---|---|---|
| 1st auction | €280,000 | €210,000 |
| 2nd auction (−25%) | €210,000 | €157,500 |
| 3rd auction (−25%) | €157,500 | €118,125 |

Two forces stop the slide in practice. Desirable lots attract multiple offers early, and a gara between five bidders in Bologna's 2024-2025 court lists routinely closed within 10% of appraisal at the first hearing. Undesirable lots keep falling for reasons the perizia usually names: occupancy, abusive works, condominium arrears. The discount is compensation for those specific risks, and pricing them is the actual skill of auction buying, the same asymmetry logic covered in the [buyer mistakes guide](/guides/mistakes-foreign-buyers-italy/).

## What does bidding actually require from a foreigner?

Bidding requires a codice fiscale, a deposit of 10% of your offered price, and a filed offer that follows the avviso to the letter: courts reject offers for a missing bollo stamp or a deposit cheque made out imprecisely. Non-residents add one logistical layer, participation, solvable by proxy or by the telematic platforms most courts now use.

The foreigner's file, in order of acquisition:

1. **Codice fiscale**: free at the consulate or Agenzia delle Entrate; the how-to is in the [codice fiscale guide](/guides/codice-fiscale-italy-property/).
2. **Italian bank relationship or assegno circolare arrangement**: the cauzione must arrive in the exact form the notice prescribes.
3. **Offer**: on the court's form, identifying the lot (RGE number), your price, and the deposit annexed.
4. **Presence**: in person, by avvocato with special power of attorney, or through the online hearing where the court sits telematically.
5. **Translator, if needed**: the hearing runs in Italian and raises come in fixed increments with minutes to respond.

Deposits of losing bidders are released at the hearing itself; the winner's rolls into the price. An offer, once filed, is irrevocable until the hearing: filing on five lots to see what sticks binds five deposits simultaneously.

## What taxes and costs apply to an auction purchase?

The tax bill mirrors an ordinary purchase, 9% registration on a second home, 2% prima casa, or VAT where the seller is a company in a taxable regime, but two costs disappear entirely: agency commission (typically 3% plus VAT) and the notary's transfer deed, because the judge's decree replaces the rogito. What remains is leaner than any private closing.

| Cost item | Ordinary purchase | Auction purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Registration tax (second home) | 9% of cadastral value (prezzo-valore) | 9%, prezzo-valore on request since 2014 |
| Agency commission | ~3% + VAT | none |
| Notary transfer deed | 1-2% | none (court decree) |
| Transfer/decree costs, delegate | n/a | €1,500-3,500 typical |
| Mortgage cancellation | seller's task | automatic in the decree |

Constitutional Court ruling 6/2014 extended prezzo-valore to forced sales, so an individual buying residential stock can elect tax on the cadastral base; on deeply discounted lots the hammer price itself is sometimes the lower figure, and the delegate computes both. The comparison mechanics sit in the [registration tax guide](/guides/italy-registration-tax-property/), and the full closing-cost stack of the conventional route, for contrast, in the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/).

A frequently missed line: condominium arrears. The buyer answers for the current and previous year's unpaid fees under the civil code's solidarity rule, and the perizia normally quantifies them; €3,000-8,000 of arrears on distressed apartment stock is ordinary, not exceptional.

## What happens when the property is occupied?

Occupancy is the single largest discount driver, and its legal texture decides everything: a debtor still living in the property is removed through the custodian within 2-6 months under the decree's own eviction force, while a lease registered before the foreclosure binds the buyer until expiry. The perizia states which case applies, and the two cases price €20,000-50,000 apart on ordinary stock.

The scenario map every bidder should hold:

| Occupant | Legal position | Practical timeline |
|---|---|---|
| Debtor / former owner | No title; decree orders release | Custodian-managed, typically 2-6 months |
| Tenant, lease registered pre-foreclosure | Lease survives; rent flows to you | Until natural expiry (4+4 residential) |
| Tenant, lease after foreclosure or unregistered | Not opposable | Removed like the debtor |
| Third party without title | No protection | Removed; timing varies by court |

Liberation practice changed materially with the 2016 credit-recovery reforms: article 560 of the procedure code now has the judge order release of the property at adjudication in the standard debtor-occupied case, executed by the custodian without a separate eviction suit, and busy courts clear most such files inside 6 months. Before the reform, buyers routinely inherited multi-year removal litigation, which is where the channel's worst folklore comes from. The surviving hard case is the genuine pre-foreclosure registered lease at market rent: a 4+4 residential contract signed and registered two years before the creditor's transcription binds the new owner for its full remaining term, and the correct response is to price the lot as a tenanted investment yielding its actual rent, not as a home with a delay.

The era of years-long stalemates has largely closed in the busier courts. Still, an occupied lot means you buy income or buy patience, never immediate keys, and the balance deadline runs regardless: your 120 days to pay do not wait for the occupant's 6 months to leave.

## Can you finance an auction purchase?

Financing is possible but the calendar is hostile: the balance falls due 60-120 days after adjudication whether or not your bank has finished underwriting, and a missed deadline forfeits the 10% deposit. Banks adhering to the ABI auction-lending protocol pre-underwrite against the lot before the hearing, which is the only sequencing that works.

The financing decision tree is short:

1. Cash available within the deadline: bid freely.
2. Mortgage pre-approved on the specific lot under the ABI protocol: bid to the pre-approved ceiling.
3. Financing hoped-for but not committed: do not bid; the deposit is the price of optimism.

Non-resident borrowers start from the tighter loan-to-value and documentation standards described in the [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-italy/), and auction lending narrows the funnel further: fewer branches handle it, and appraisal-versus-hammer-price gaps confuse loan-to-value math. For sub-€150,000 lots, the segment covered in the [under €200k guide](/guides/buy-property-italy-under-200000/), most successful foreign bidders simply pay cash.

**Insider tip:** where the mortgage matters, ask the bank for its delibera before the hearing naming the RGE number of the lot. A generic pre-approval letter is not a commitment to fund this auction on this calendar, and the difference surfaces exactly when the 120-day clock is already running.

## Is an Italian auction worth it for a foreign buyer?

An auction rewards a bidder who can price specific, named risks and punishes one shopping for a generic discount: the channel routinely delivers 25-50% below appraisal precisely because the perizia lists problems a conventional sale would have forced the seller to fix. For a prepared buyer with cash and counsel, the arithmetic is often unbeatable; for a first-time buyer abroad, the conventional market's warranties are worth their premium.

Auction economics summarised against the ordinary route on a €200,000 target budget:

| Factor | Auction | Conventional purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Entry price vs value | commonly −25% or deeper | market price, ~8% average negotiated discount |
| Transaction costs saved | agency + notary deed (~4-5%) | n/a |
| Seller warranties | none | civil-code guarantees |
| Possession certainty | perizia-dependent | vacant at rogito by default |
| Capital timeline | 10% now, 100% in ≤120 days | staged via compromesso |

Auction arithmetic on a concrete Bologna case shows both edges at once: a €280,000-appraised apartment taken at the second hearing for €165,000 carries no agency fee (€6,000 saved) and no notary deed (€3,000 saved), pays 9% registration on a €138,000 cadastral base, and lands at roughly €182,000 all-in, 35% under appraisal. The same lot with a pre-foreclosure lease running 3 more years at €750 a month is not cheap at all for a buyer who needed a home, and exactly right for one who wanted a 5% yielding tenanted asset. The channel does not sell bargains; it sells accurately priced problems to buyers who can read them, and the two Bologna outcomes above are the same lot separated only by one paragraph of the perizia on occupancy status.

The honest filter is a checklist, not a mood: read the perizia yourself with a translator, price the occupancy scenario, verify condominium arrears, confirm the funds' timeline, and compare the all-in result against simply negotiating harder on the open market with the tactics in the [offer and negotiation guide](/guides/italy-property-offer-negotiation/). When the auction still wins after that comparison, it tends to win big.
