The hard part of moving from the United States to Italy is not finding a house. It is that the steps have to happen in an order most people discover backwards: you need an Italian address before you can apply for the visa, you need the visa before you can enter to stay, and you need to be inside Italy for only 8 days before the next deadline lands.

**Quick answer:** a US passport buys 90 days in any 180-day period and no right to stay. To live in Italy you apply at an Italian consulate in the United States, before travelling, for Elective Residence if you live on passive income, or the Digital Nomad Visa if you work remotely for a foreign employer. Both require a documented Italian address first. Allow 6 to 12 months end to end, and expect to keep filing a US tax return every year afterwards.

## Which visa can an American actually get?

Three routes carry almost every American who moves, and they are separated by where your money comes from rather than how much of it there is. Get that distinction wrong and the file is refused regardless of the balance in the account.

| Route | Money must come from | Can you work? | Typical applicant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elective Residence | Pensions, dividends, rents, annuities | No, and any work breaks the terms | Retired, or living on capital |
| Digital Nomad Visa | Remote salary or client contracts, foreign employer | Yes, for a non-Italian employer | Working age, employed or freelance |
| Citizenship by descent | Not an income test at all | Yes, it grants a passport | Qualifying Italian ancestry |

Elective Residence carries a published floor of €31,160 a year in passive income for a single applicant, and consulates in practice look for a good deal more before approving. The bar that refuses most files is not the amount but the word passive: remote work income presented as passive is the single most common reason for rejection, and it is not a technicality the consulate will overlook.

The Digital Nomad route runs the opposite test. It exists precisely for people who work, expects roughly €28,000 a year in documented remote earnings, six months of contract history, and a degree or three years of relevant experience. Our [digital nomad guide](/guides/digital-nomad-italy-property-guide/) covers the document set, and the [elective residence guide](/guides/italy-elective-residence-visa-property/) covers the passive income evidence.

Citizenship by descent is a different animal. It is not a permit, it is recognition of a citizenship you may already hold through an ancestor. The rules were reformed in 2025 and the restrictions were upheld by the Constitutional Court in March 2026, which narrowed eligibility to an Italian-born parent or grandparent. Our guide to [Italian citizenship by descent](/guides/italy-citizenship-by-descent/) sets out who still qualifies. Check it first, because it makes every other route unnecessary.

## Do you need an Italian address before the visa?

Accommodation comes before the interview, not after it, and this is the sequencing that surprises almost everyone. The consulate wants documented housing in Italy at the point you apply, which means either a registered lease running at least 12 months or a property in your name.

That creates an uncomfortable decision. Signing a 12 month lease on a town you have visited twice, from 4,000 miles away, is a real commitment. Buying is a larger one. The three ways people resolve it:

- **Rent long, remotely.** Cheapest and reversible. The lease must be registered with the Agenzia delle Entrate, because an unregistered private agreement will not satisfy a consulate.
- **Buy first.** Removes the annual renewal question and satisfies the requirement permanently, but commits capital before you have lived there. A purchase runs 2% registration tax on a primary residence bought from a private seller and 9% otherwise, plus notary and agency costs.
- **Hospitality declaration.** A resident, usually family, formally hosts you. Free, and viable only if you have someone.

What no route does is turn the property into the visa. Italy has no property-purchase Golden Visa, and any adviser offering one is describing something that cannot be delivered. The house proves where you will sleep. It proves nothing about your right to be there. Our guide on [buying property in Italy as a foreigner](/guides/buy-property-italy-foreigner/) covers the purchase itself, and the [reciprocity rule](/guides/italy-reciprocity-property-foreigners/) confirms that US citizens have full buying rights regardless of visa status.

## What are the actual steps, in order?

Nine steps, and the order is not negotiable. The two that create most of the pain are the consular appointment, which you cannot control, and the 8 day window after arrival, which you can.

| # | Step | Where | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Choose the route and test the income against it | Home | 2 to 8 weeks |
| 2 | Secure Italian accommodation, registered | Italy, remotely | 4 to 12 weeks |
| 3 | Obtain a codice fiscale | Consulate or Italy | 1 to 4 weeks |
| 4 | Book the consular appointment | US consulate for your state | 2 weeks to 3 months |
| 5 | Attend the interview with the full file | US consulate | 1 day |
| 6 | Visa decision and passport sticker | US consulate | 30 to 90 days |
| 7 | Enter Italy | Any Schengen entry point | Clock starts |
| 8 | File the permesso di soggiorno kit | Post office, then Questura | Within 8 days of entry |
| 9 | Register with the comune, Anagrafe | Local town hall | Within 45 days of the vigile visit |

Step 4 is the one to start early. Which consulate covers you is fixed by your state of residence and cannot be chosen, and appointment availability varies enormously between posts. People routinely lose two months here for no reason other than booking late.

Step 8 catches those who arrive, take a fortnight to settle and then think about paperwork. The receipt from the post office kit is what proves your status while the card is produced, so keep it somewhere you can find it at a police check or a bank counter.

## What happens to your US taxes?

Your filing obligation crosses the Atlantic with you and never lapses. The United States taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residence, so from year one you file a US return and, once you are tax resident in Italy, an Italian one as well.

Italian tax residency generally begins when you spend 183 or more days in Italy in a calendar year, or when your centre of vital interests moves there. From that point Italy taxes your worldwide income through IRPEF, and you rely on the US-Italy treaty and the foreign tax credit so the same income is not taxed twice. The mechanics are not automatic and they are not something to work out yourself in April.

Two further points that catch Americans specifically. Foreign account reporting obligations attach to Italian bank accounts once balances cross the thresholds, and some Italian banks are reluctant to open accounts for US clients because of the compliance burden, so start that conversation months before the notary needs funds. And if you keep and let a US property while living in Italy, it becomes reportable in both systems; our guide on [US tax on Italian rental property](/guides/us-tax-italy-rental-property/) covers the mirror-image case.

Budget for a preparer who genuinely handles both jurisdictions. This is not the year to economise on advice.

## What happens after the first permit?

The first permesso is short and the renewals are what turn a move into a life. An Elective Residence permit typically issues for 12 months and renews for 24 months at a time, each renewal repeating the income and accommodation tests, so the qualifying income has to be durable rather than a one-year snapshot.

Two longer horizons matter for planning. After 5 years of continuous legal residence you can apply for the EU long-term residence permit, which is open-ended and removes the renewal cycle. After 10 years of continuous legal residence a non-EU citizen can apply for Italian citizenship by naturalisation, subject to language and income conditions. Marriage to an Italian citizen and descent both run on shorter and separate tracks.

Plan the property decision against the 5 year mark rather than the first 12 months. People who buy in year one on the assumption they will renew, and then fail an income test in year three, are the ones who sell in a hurry.

## What does the first year cost?

The recurring cost of living in Italy is lower than in most US metros, which is well covered in our [cost of living guide](/guides/cost-of-living-italy/). The first year is the exception, because a stack of one-off costs lands at once.

| One-off item | Rough scale |
|---|---|
| Visa application and document legalisation | Low hundreds, per person |
| Shipping a household, or replacing it | The largest discretionary variable |
| Deposit and agency fee on a lease | Commonly 3 to 4 months of rent combined |
| Furnishing, if unfurnished | Italian unfurnished often means no kitchen at all |
| Codice fiscale, translations, commercialista | Low hundreds to low thousands |
| Purchase costs if buying | Registration tax at 2% or 9%, plus notary and agency |
| Car, if the town requires one | Purchase, insurance, annual roadworthiness test |

Many people ship far more than they need. Italian apartments are smaller than American houses, doorways and lifts are narrower, and 220 volt wiring makes most US appliances useless. The cheaper answer is usually to bring what is irreplaceable and buy the rest locally.

The offsetting number is healthcare. Once you hold a permit and register with the comune you can enrol in the national health service, and for most American households that single change removes the largest recurring line in the old budget.

## Are the "get paid to move to Italy" schemes real?

The headlines are real; the offer behind them is not what the headline implies. One-euro house programmes are auctions of derelict buildings in depopulating towns, and the euro is the reserve price, not the cost. What actually binds you is the renovation commitment: a deadline, usually 1 to 3 years, and a deposit held against completion, typically several thousand euros, forfeited if you miss it. Renovation of a stone shell runs into tens of thousands at minimum.

Some southern comuni and regions have run relocation incentives paying new residents to settle, and a few have been genuine. They are small, conditional on residency and often on opening a business, and they change or close without much notice. They are a reason to look harder at a town you already liked. They are not a reason to move to one you have never seen.

The reliable version of this idea is simpler: property in inland Molise, Basilicata and Calabria is cheap on the open market without any scheme attached, frequently at €400 to €900 per m² for a habitable house. That is a real discount, available to anyone, with no deadline attached.

## Where do Americans actually settle?

Three patterns dominate, and they map to the three visa routes more closely than to any preference about scenery.

**Cities, for the working.** Milan, Rome, Bologna and Turin, because they have international schools, airports with US connections, hospitals and enough English-speaking professional infrastructure to make a first year survivable. Turin is the value case: a major northern city at roughly half Milan's housing cost.

**Small towns in Tuscany, Umbria and Le Marche, for the retired.** The classic choice, and the one where the gap between visiting and living is widest. Winters are quiet, the car is not optional, and the nearest specialist hospital may be an hour away.

Our guide to [the best places to live in Italy](/guides/best-places-to-live-italy/) scores these against hospital access and flight connections rather than scenery.

**The south, for the budget-led.** Puglia, Abruzzo, Calabria and Sicily, where the same money buys several times the space. The trade is flight connections, which for someone who returns to the US three times a year is a genuine recurring cost rather than an inconvenience.

**Pros of the move, as people actually report them.** Healthcare costs collapse, food and daily life are cheaper and better, public transport works, and space that is unaffordable in a US metro is ordinary here.

**Cons, as people actually report them.** Bureaucracy is slow and in Italian, local salaries are far below US levels if you intend to earn there, banking is harder with a US passport, and the first winter in an uninsulated house changes people's minds about stone farmhouses.

## Who this is for: three scenarios that behave differently

The same move splits into three quite different projects depending on where the money comes from and how long you intend to stay.

| Scenario | Route | Housing decision | The thing that goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retired couple, pension income, permanent move | Elective Residence | Buy early; the property also settles the accommodation requirement for every renewal | Underestimating winter running costs in an old rural house |
| Working remotely, 35 to 50, testing Italy for 2 years | Digital Nomad Visa | Rent on a registered 12 month lease; buy only after the first renewal | Signing a purchase before knowing which city works |
| Part-year owner, keeping a US base | No visa needed if under 90 days in any 180 | Buy freely; US citizens have full purchase rights under reciprocity | Drifting over 90 days and creating an overstay |

The third case is the one people misread most often. You do not need any visa to own Italian property, and buying does not extend the 90 day allowance by a single day. Ownership and immigration status are two separate systems that never touch.

### Red flags and what to check before you commit

- **An adviser offering residency through property.** Italy has no property-purchase Golden Visa. This is the clearest signal to walk away.
- **An unregistered lease.** A private agreement not registered with the Agenzia delle Entrate will not satisfy a consulate, and you will discover this at the interview.
- **Remote work presented as passive income.** The most common single cause of Elective Residence refusals, and consulates check.
- **Booking the consular appointment last.** It is the longest and least controllable step. Book it first and build the file around the date.
- **Assuming the 8 day permesso window is advisory.** It is not, and missing it creates a problem that takes months to unwind.
- **No US and Italy tax preparer lined up before the move.** Year one is the expensive year to improvise.

Resolve the visa question before the property question, because the property that suits you depends on which route you qualify for and on what your consulate needs to see.
