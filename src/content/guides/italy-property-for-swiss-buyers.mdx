**Quick answer:** Switzerland exempts your Italian property from tax and then charges for it twice indirectly. The house raises the rate applied to your Swiss income and your Swiss wealth, and it shifts part of your mortgage interest into a foreign allocation where Switzerland will not deduct it. Both effects arrive even though the Italian house is never itself taxed in Switzerland.

<TldrBlock text="Foreign real estate exempt but rate-determining, on income and wealth · debt interest allocated proportionally by asset location · a cash Italian purchase still reduces the Swiss deduction · Eigenmietwert abolished by referendum 28.09.2025, in force 01.01.2029 · Swiss citizens exempt from Italian reciprocity"/>

The Swiss treatment of foreign property is described in one sentence in most guides: exempt with progression. That sentence is accurate and it conceals the two mechanisms that actually cost Swiss owners money, one of which fires even when the Italian purchase is made entirely in cash.

## What does exemption with progression cost in practice?

Swiss residents are taxed on worldwide income and wealth in principle, and real estate abroad is then carved out. The carve-out is not a deletion: the exempt income and the exempt asset value are used to establish the rate, which is then applied to what remains taxable.

Because both Swiss income tax and Swiss wealth tax are progressive, and because the wealth tax is cantonal, the size of the effect depends on where the owner lives and how far up the scale they already sit. A household near the top of a canton's scale sees very little change. A household in the middle can find an Italian purchase moves them into a materially higher band on income they earned in Zurich.

This is the same relief method Germany applies, with one decisive difference. Germany removes rental income from EU and EEA property from the progression calculation entirely, so a German owner sees no rate effect at all. Switzerland has no such carve-out, so the Italian house does what a property anywhere in the world would do.

| Country of the owner | Method on Italian rent | Rate effect at home |
|---|---|---|
| Switzerland | Exempt | Yes, on income and wealth |
| Germany | Exempt | None, EU property carved out |

## Why does a cash purchase in Italy raise the tax on your Swiss house?

This is the mechanism Swiss buyers most often meet after the fact, and it has nothing to do with how the Italian purchase was financed.

Swiss practice allocates debt and debt interest across the taxpayer's assets in proportion to where those assets are located. The allocation follows the balance sheet, not the loan agreement. If 20% of your total assets sit in Italy, then roughly 20% of your debt interest is allocated to Italy, and because Italian income is exempt in Switzerland, that share of interest is not deductible against Swiss income.

The consequence is counterintuitive and real. A Swiss owner with a mortgage on their home in Bern, who buys an Italian apartment for cash, has increased the foreign share of their assets without adding any debt. Part of the Bern interest is now allocated abroad. The Swiss deduction shrinks because of a purchase that involved no borrowing at all.

- The allocation is by asset location and value, not by which property secured which loan
- Buying in Italy with an Italian mortgage adds the debt to the foreign side too, which changes the arithmetic rather than removing it
- The effect scales with the size of the Italian purchase relative to total assets
- **Risk:** a buyer modelling the Italian purchase in isolation will not see this cost at all, because it appears on the Swiss return as a reduced deduction rather than as a new charge

## How does the 2029 system change rewrite the borrowing decision?

On 28 September 2025 Swiss voters approved the reform of residential property taxation by 57.7%, and the Federal Council has set entry into force for 1 January 2029, with a transition period running to the end of 2028.

Two elements matter to a Swiss owner of Italian property. The imputed rental value on owner-occupied property is abolished, which removes a notional income that had also fed the rate calculation. And the deduction for debt interest is largely withdrawn, with narrow exceptions preserved for let property under a quota-restrictive method and for a first purchase of owner-occupied housing.

That second element removes the main Swiss tax reason for carrying a mortgage. Owners who structured their affairs around interest deductibility, which is a very large share of Swiss homeowners, are looking at a different calculation from 2029, and the allocation problem described above matters less once there is less deduction left to allocate.

The planning implication for an Italian purchase is about sequencing rather than avoidance. A buyer completing in 2026 or 2027 is operating under the current rules with the reform visible on the horizon, and the debt structure that suits the next 3 years may not suit the following decade. Cantons have also been given a constitutional basis to levy a specific property tax on predominantly self-occupied secondary residences, which is a Swiss cost on Swiss second homes rather than on Italian ones, but it belongs in the same review.

- Model the mortgage decision under both regimes before fixing a long term
- The exceptions to the interest deduction are narrow, and a holiday home is not a first purchase of owner-occupied housing
- Transitional treatment for existing arrangements is the detail to confirm with a Swiss adviser as 2028 approaches

## Can you use pension capital to buy in Italy?

No, and this is the constraint that most often decides the size of a Swiss purchase rather than its location.

Swiss occupational pension capital and pillar 3a savings can be drawn early or pledged to finance owner-occupied residential property, which is how a large share of Swiss households assemble a deposit. The permission is specific: it covers property in Switzerland used as the owner's own main residence. A holiday home does not qualify, and a property outside Switzerland does not qualify.

The practical effect is that a Swiss buyer's largest pool of accessible capital is unavailable for exactly this purchase. A household that could raise several hundred thousand francs towards a flat in Lausanne can raise none of it towards a house on Lake Maggiore, and has to fund the Italian purchase from ordinary savings, from an Italian mortgage, or by increasing borrowing against the Swiss home.

That last route reconnects to the allocation problem. Raising a Swiss mortgage to buy in Italy increases Swiss debt while increasing the foreign share of assets, so the interest on the new borrowing is partly allocated abroad and partly non-deductible from the start.

- Pension withdrawal and pledging are available only for owner-occupied Swiss property, and neither route extends to a second home located abroad
- Increasing the Swiss mortgage to fund an Italian purchase produces partly non-deductible interest immediately
- An Italian mortgage keeps the debt on the same side of the border as the asset, which is cleaner for the allocation even where the headline rate is less attractive than a Swiss lender would offer on the same security

## How much does your canton change the answer?

More than any other variable on this page, because both the income tax rate and the wealth tax that the Italian house influences are set cantonally.

Wealth tax rates differ by a wide margin between cantons, and the progression curves differ as much as the headline rates. In a canton with a shallow curve and a low top rate, adding an Italian property to the rate-determining base moves the result very little. In a canton with a steep curve, the same house can shift the household into a noticeably higher band on assets that never left Switzerland.

Income tax works the same way with an added municipal layer, so two households with identical Italian properties and identical Swiss salaries can face materially different Swiss consequences purely by commune of residence.

| Variable | Set by | Effect on the Italian house |
|---|---|---|
| Income tax rate | Federal, cantonal and communal | Determines the cost of the progression effect |
| Wealth tax rate and curve | Cantonal | Determines the cost of the rate-determining asset |
| Debt interest allocation | Federal practice, applied cantonally | Determines the lost deduction |

- **Insider tip:** a household considering both an intercantonal move and an Italian purchase should model them together, because the same Italian house has a different Swiss cost in each canton
- The allocation rules are consistent nationally; the rates they interact with are not
- Ask the cantonal administration for the rate-determination treatment in writing where the sums are large, since practice on foreign property valuation varies
- Declare the Italian property from the first return in which you own it, because a late correction reopens earlier years rather than starting fresh from the current one

## What is genuinely easier for a Swiss buyer?

Two things, and they are worth stating because the tax picture above is unusually intricate.

The purchase itself carries no nationality step. Swiss citizens fall outside article 16 reciprocity through the free movement agreement signed in 1999, so the notary has no table to consult and the file proceeds exactly as an Italian buyer's would. The [purchase sequence](/guides/how-to-buy-italy-property-step-by-step/) applies unchanged.

Time at the property is unrestricted. A Swiss passport holder faces no Schengen 90-day limit, which separates them from every other non-EU buyer in this cluster and makes an Italian property a genuine second home rather than a place they may visit for a quarter of the year. For owners near the border, which describes a great many Swiss purchases around the lakes, the practical effect is that the Italian house can be used most weekends of the year without a calculation. The [Lake Como guide](/guides/lake-como-property-investment-guide/) covers the market that geography creates.

## Which Swiss buyer scenarios does this change?

**The border-region weekender.** Buying within driving distance, using the house heavily, letting rarely. The exemption with progression costs little because there is little Italian income, but the wealth-tax rate effect and the interest allocation both apply. This profile should look at the allocation question specifically, since it is the one cost they will otherwise never see.

**The mortgaged Swiss homeowner buying for cash in Italy.** The most exposed to the allocation mechanism and the least likely to have been warned about it. The decision framework is to compute the reduced Swiss deduction as part of the Italian purchase price rather than as a separate annual annoyance.

**The buyer letting the Italian property.** Italian tax decides the actual bill, as our [cedolare secca guide](/guides/cedolare-secca-italy-rental-property/) sets out, while the Swiss effect stays indirect. Note that let property retains an interest deduction route under the quota method after 2029, so a property intended for letting sits differently from one intended for personal use.

**The buyer approaching 2029.** Everyone, eventually. The reform is legislated and dated, the transition runs to the end of 2028, and a mortgage fixed for 10 years today spans both regimes.

For the annual Italian holding cost that applies regardless of any of this, the [IMU guide](/guides/imu-property-tax-italy/) sets out the second-home bands, the [prima casa comparison](/guides/italy-prima-casa-vs-second-home-tax/) explains why a Swiss resident pays them, and the [selling guide](/guides/selling-property-italy-foreigner/) covers the Italian side of an eventual exit. What an Italian lender will advance to a non-resident is in the [mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-italy/).
