Quick answer: transcribing the preliminary contract converts your private promise into a published right: from the filing date, later deeds, later mortgages and the seller's creditors rank behind you, at an all-in cost of €1,700-3,200 and subject to one unforgiving clock, the protection lapses if the final deed is not transcribed within 1 year of the agreed completion date and always within 3 years of the filing. For long gaps, off-plan tails and doubtful sellers it is the strongest shield Italian law sells a buyer; for a clean 60-day closing it is usually money spent on a risk the caparra already prices. This guide covers the mechanics, the lapse rule, the cost stack and the decision, building on the [compromesso guide](/guides/compromesso-italy-property-contract/)'s contract basics.

## What does transcribing a preliminare actually change?

Transcription is publicity, and publicity is 100% of the difference: an ordinary compromesso, even tax-registered, binds only its parties, so a second buyer, a lending bank or a judgment creditor can acquire rights on the property without knowing you exist. A transcribed preliminare under article 2645-bis enters the same public registers as deeds, and priority runs strictly by filing date.

The two instruments side by side:

| Feature | Private compromesso | Preliminare trascritto |
|---|---|---|
| Binds the seller to sell | yes | yes |
| Caparra machinery (forfeit / double) | yes | yes |
| Visible to third parties | no | yes, from filing |
| Beats a later sale to another buyer | no (damages only) | yes (their deed ranks behind) |
| Beats later mortgages and attachments | no | yes |
| Form required | private writing | notarial deed or authenticated signatures |
| Typical all-in cost | €0-500 (registration) | €1,700-3,200 |

Foreign buyers meet the register's logic here for the first time, and it rewards early understanding: everything in Italian property that matters to third parties, deeds, mortgages, attachments, your preliminare, lives or dies by its transcription date, and the conservatoria's queue never re-orders for fairness.

The mechanism's legal core is rank: Italian property rights order themselves by transcription date, not by contract date, and article 2645-bis lets a future right occupy a place in that queue years before the deed exists. Everything else in this guide, the costs, the clocks, the scenarios, is arithmetic on that single privilege.

## Which gap risks does the registered preliminary defeat?

The months between compromesso and rogito are the exposed season of an Italian purchase: a caparra of €30,000-60,000 is committed, the [balance is in motion](/guides/transferring-money-to-italy-property/), and the property's register can still change beneath you. Transcription closes the 4 classic gap events, each with a documented history of ruining unregistered buyers.

1. **The double sale**: a seller signs with you privately, then deeds to a better offer; without transcription your remedy is suing a person who may be spending your damages already, while with it the second buyer's deed ranks behind your filing and your court transfer prevails.
2. **The fresh mortgage**: a seller in quiet difficulty pledges the property after your compromesso; unregistered, you complete into someone else's security or walk; registered, the bank's hypothec ranks behind you.
3. **The creditor's attachment**: a pignoramento transcribed before your deed would normally sweep you into someone else's foreclosure; a prior-ranked preliminare holds your place.
4. **The insolvency scenario**: within limits, the registered buyer's position resists the seller's bankruptcy machinery, particularly for main-home purchases at fair value, where the special protections are strongest.

Double-sale case law built this article: before 1997, when 2645-bis entered the code, Italian reports carried a steady stream of buyers who had paid 10-30% deposits on private compromessi and watched the property deed to someone else, holding nothing but a damages claim against a seller who had often spent both deposits. The legislature's fix was queue-based and total: from the filing date the registered future buyer outranks every later acquirer, and the second deed, valid as a contract, transfers a property already burdened by your priority. Practitioners date the near-disappearance of classic double-sale fraud in registered files to exactly that reform, which is why that €200 transcription tax line remains, euro for euro, the single most protective charge in the entire Italian closing stack.

The remedy that makes rank valuable is specific performance: under article 2932 a court can transfer the property by judgment when the seller will not, and for a transcribed preliminare that judgment relates back to the original filing's priority. The unregistered buyer holding the same contract holds a claim for money against a counterparty whose money may be gone; the registered buyer holds the house's queue position. Italian practice prices that difference at €1,700-3,200, and in the scenarios above it is the cheapest number in the file.

**Insider tip:** transcription also quietly upgrades your diligence calendar: because the notary must receive or authenticate the preliminare, title checks that normally happen weeks before the rogito happen at the compromesso instead, and a seller whose file cannot survive that early scrutiny exits the transaction while your caparra is still small.

## How does the 1-year/3-year lapse rule work?

The shield has an expiry design that forgives nothing: the transcription's effects cease if the final deed (or a judicial demand under 2932) is not itself transcribed within 1 year of the completion date the preliminary agreed, and in every case within 3 years of the preliminary's own transcription, whichever bites first.

Worked Florence calendar on a renovation-delayed purchase:

| Event | Date | Clock state |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminare transcribed | 1 March 2026 | 3-year outer limit: 1 March 2029 |
| Agreed rogito date | 30 September 2026 | 1-year inner limit: 30 September 2027 |
| Seller's works overrun | spring 2027 | still inside both clocks |
| Rogito transcribed | 15 September 2027 | protected, 15 days to spare |
| Same deal closing 15 October 2027 | | naked: priority lost, ordinary rules apply |

Postponements are where the Florence table's margin evaporates in real files: sellers' renovations overrun by quarters, probate schedules slip, and each informal extension quietly spends the buffer between the agreed date and the inner clock's expiry.

Three practice rules from that arithmetic:

- **Diarise both dates at signature** and treat the inner one as a project deadline, because the lapse is automatic: no notice, no grace, no renewal by conduct.
- **Amend formally when timelines move**: a notarised postponement of the completion date resets the 1-year clock (never the 3-year one), while a handshake extension moves the deal and abandons the shield.
- **File the 2932 demand before the clocks die** where a seller stalls: the demand's transcription preserves rank exactly as the deed would.

Lapse statistics live mostly in cautionary tales because the rule is self-executing: no registry officer warns you at month 11, no renewal filing exists, and the Cassazione has enforced both windows literally, including against sympathetic buyers whose delays were entirely their sellers' fault. The practical planning consequence is that a transcribed preliminare converts your entire closing calendar into a legal deadline with exactly 2 dates, and professional practice treats the inner 1-year clock the way lawyers treat limitation periods: diarised twice, reviewed at every postponement, and cured by a notarised amendment (€300-800) whenever the completion date moves by more than weeks. Buyers who spend €2,500 on the shield and then let a handshake extension kill it at month 13 are the recurring characters of this statute's case law.

The outer 3-year wall is the structural limit for slow projects: land-plus-permits stories and phased developments that cannot promise a deed inside 3 years need different protection architecture, the fideiussione regime of the [off-plan guide](/guides/italy-off-plan-property-guide/) or the 10-year shield of [rent-to-buy](/guides/rent-to-buy-italy-affitto-con-riscatto/), both designed for horizons the preliminare's clocks refuse.

## What does the transcription cost, line by line?

The cost stack is fixed-heavy and caparra-sensitive: notarial fees of €1,000-2,500 for receiving or authenticating the contract, €200 fixed registration tax plus 0.5% proportional on the caparra confirmatoria (creditable against the final registration bill), €200 transcription tax and roughly €155 of stamps, landing most files between €1,700 and €3,200 all-in.

| Line | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Notary (preliminare) | €1,000-2,500 | scale with price and complexity |
| Registration tax, fixed | €200 | due on any registered preliminare anyway |
| Registration on caparra | 0.5% | credited at the rogito against registration due |
| Transcription tax | €200 | the publicity itself |
| Stamps and sundries | ≈€155 | |
| **Typical all-in** | **€1,700-3,200** | on a €300,000-500,000 purchase |

One line in the stack is refundable in kind rather than cash: the notary who received your preliminare typically discounts the rogito fee for the same file, since the title work was largely done at the first act, a saving of €300-800 that most quotes only reveal when asked.

Two softeners improve the real economics. The 0.5% on the caparra is a prepayment, not a cost: on a €40,000 caparra the €200 proportional charge nets off the final bill, so the true incremental spend is the notary plus fixed items. And the notarial involvement replaces spend you might incur anyway: buyers who would engage a lawyer to redraft an agency's compromesso (€500-1,500, per the [offer guide](/guides/italy-property-offer-negotiation/)'s advice) are buying much of that review inside the notary's preliminare fee.

Currency of the comparison matters to foreign files specifically: the €1,700-3,200 stack does not scale with price the way percentages do, so on a €700,000 Florence purchase it is 0.3% of price protecting a €70,000-105,000 caparra, while on a €150,000 rural file the same stack is 1.5% of price protecting €15,000, arithmetic that quietly explains why transcription practice concentrates in the upper half of the market and in every professional investor's file regardless of size.

The comparison that decides most files: €1,700-3,200 against the caparra it protects. At a 10% caparra on €400,000, the shield costs 4-8% of the deposit sitting exposed through the gap, which reads as obviously cheap insurance at 6 months of gap and obviously excessive at 6 weeks.

## When should a buyer insist on transcription, and when skip it?

The decision reduces to gap length times seller risk: transcription is close to mandatory where the closing sits 4+ months away or the seller's solvency carries any doubt, and close to pointless on a 6-week resale from a pensioner selling their second home to fund the first. The scenarios sort cleanly.

| Scenario | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Off-plan tail beyond the fideiussione | transcribe | long gap + developer balance-sheet risk |
| 6-12 month gap (works, probate, your sale) | transcribe | the gap is the risk |
| Seller a company, or visible debt signals | transcribe | mortgage/attachment scenarios live here |
| Caparra above 15% or early occupation | transcribe | exposure justifies the fee |
| Clean 45-90 day resale, solvent private seller | usually skip | caparra rules price the residual risk |
| Rent-to-buy structure | own regime | 10-year shield under the 2014 rules |

Off-plan interaction deserves precision because the two shields cover different failures: the mandatory fideiussione refunds 100% of advances if the developer fails, but it does not stop the developer's bank from consolidating its site-wide mortgage ahead of your unit, nor a corporate seller in difficulty from deeding your future flat elsewhere as part of a restructuring. A transcribed preliminare closes exactly those register-side gaps, at its usual €1,700-3,200, which is why careful off-plan buyers of units above the €300,000 line increasingly choose to run both instruments together: the statutory guarantee protects the money paid, the register transcription protects the queue position itself, and the 3-year outer clock is then actively managed against the delivery schedule using the notarised amendment machinery described above.

Buyer profiles repeat across those rows predictably: the off-plan investor and the buyer funding a seller's long renovation are the natural clients of article 2645-bis; the retired couple buying a Florence apartment with keys in 60 days are not, and an agent pushing notarial costs onto that second file deserves the question «which gap risk, exactly?». Between the poles, the tiebreaker is the seller's reaction to the request itself: acceptance costs a solvent seller almost nothing, so resistance is data, the same negotiating logic that runs through every [condition conversation](/guides/italy-property-offer-negotiation/).

Sequenced into the wider purchase, the registered preliminare sits exactly where the [compromesso](/guides/compromesso-italy-property-contract/) would: same caparra mechanics, same conditions, same [due diligence](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/) feeding its clauses, with the notarial form and the register filing layered on top and the [notaio](/guides/notaio-italy-property-role/)'s early involvement as the bonus. It is not a different road to the rogito: it is the same road with the right of way published, and for the files that need it, no private drafting substitutes for that queue position.
