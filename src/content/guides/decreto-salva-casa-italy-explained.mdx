## Salva Casa: a rule change, not a pardon

**Quick answer:** The Decreto Salva Casa is a 2024 reform of the Italian building code, made by decree-law 69/2024 and converted by law 105/2024. It widened the tolerances inside which a building still counts as legitimate, created a lighter regularisation route for minor breaches, and relaxed some habitability requirements for existing buildings. It is not an amnesty. It does not forgive a building erected without a title, and inside a protected area it helps much less than the headlines suggested.

For a foreign buyer the practical consequence is narrow and useful: a property that a surveyor would have called unsellable before 2024 may now be certifiable, and the test is arithmetic rather than judgement.

## The tolerances, which is the part that changes outcomes

The old rule was a flat 2% on the permitted measurements. Anything beyond that was a deviation to be dealt with, which on a small flat meant that a few centimetres of wall in the wrong place created a formal problem.

The reform replaced the flat figure with bands that scale inversely with the size of the unit, on the sensible reasoning that the same absolute error is proportionally larger on a small property.

| Useful surface of the unit | Tolerance |
|---|---|
| Under 60 sqm | 6% |
| Up to 100 sqm | 5% |
| 100 to 300 sqm | 4% |
| 300 to 500 sqm | 3% |
| Over 500 sqm | 2% |

The tolerance applies to height, setbacks, volume, covered area and the other measured parameters, and it covers works **completed before 24 May 2024**. Inside the band the divergence is not a building violation, which means a professional can certify [stato legittimo](/guides/abuso-edilizio-italy-building-compliance/) notwithstanding it.

That last sentence is the whole commercial point. A certified stato legittimo is what a notaio wants to see and what a lender's valuer wants to see, and a great many older properties that failed the flat 2% test pass a 5% or 6% one.

### What the bands look like on a real flat

Take a 75 sqm flat in a 1960s block, the sort of property foreign buyers acquire in Italian cities constantly. A survey finds that the internal layout diverges from the approved drawing: a wall in a different position, and a measured area 3 sqm larger than the drawing shows.

Under the old flat threshold, tolerance on 75 sqm was 1.5 sqm. A 3 sqm divergence was double the allowance, so the flat had a formal irregularity that had to be regularised before it could be sold cleanly, if it could be regularised at all.

Under the bands, a unit up to 100 sqm carries 5%, which is 3.75 sqm. The same divergence now falls inside the tolerance. Nothing about the flat changed. The certification did, and with it the mortgage, the deed and the price.

That is why this provision, rather than any of the others, is the one worth checking first on an older property.

## Article 36-bis, and the end of the double test for small breaches

The second change is more technical and matters just as much.

Italian law had long required **doppia conformità** for regularisation: the work had to comply with the planning and building rules in force when it was carried out **and** with those in force when the application was made. Rules change over decades, so a great many minor breaches were unregularisable not because anyone objected to them but because the two moments never lined up.

The reform left that test in place under article 36 for serious abuses, and created a new **article 36-bis** for minor ones, splitting the test:

- **Building rules** as they stood when the work was carried out
- **Planning rules** as they stand when the application is made

Article 36-bis covers partial deviations from a permit or a SCIA, works done where a SCIA was required and never filed, and essential variations from the title. Article 36, with the full double test, still governs construction with no title at all and anything radically incompatible with the planning regime.

For a buyer this converts a category. Divergences that a geometra would previously have described as permanently irregular are now, in many cases, regularisable on payment. That is a costed number to negotiate over rather than a reason to withdraw.

## Habitability, and the flats that were not flats

The third change is the least discussed and the most relevant to anyone buying small urban property to let or to restore.

Italian habitability standards descended from a 1975 decree that set a minimum internal height of 2.70 m and minimum floor areas that ruled out much of the older housing stock. Under the reform, for the recovery of existing buildings, a professional can certify a unit habitable at a minimum internal height of **2.40 m**, and at **20 sqm** for a single occupant or **28 sqm** for two, subject to conditions including compensating quality requirements.

The relaxations took effect on **28 July 2024** and are expressly **not retroactive**: they are also framed as transitional, pending a health ministry decree setting performance-based requirements.

Two more provisions belong here. Change of use within the same functional category was simplified, which matters where a buyer wants an accessory space counted as living space. And removable glazed enclosures on balconies and porticoes were brought within the works needing no permission, which regularised a very common alteration.

## Change of use, and the space you already own

The provision that gets least attention is the one most likely to make a buyer money.

Italian planning sorts uses into functional categories: residential, tourist accommodation, productive, commercial, rural. Moving a property between categories has always been a substantial permission question. Moving it **within** a category, and converting accessory space into usable space, was hedged with municipal rules that varied unpredictably and often blocked changes nobody had a reason to object to.

The reform simplified this, with the explicit policy aim of getting more use out of buildings that already exist rather than consuming more land. In practice it widens what can be done with the parts of a property that were never counted as living space: a taverna, a sottotetto, a ground-floor deposito, a garage that has not held a car in thirty years.

The effect combines with the habitability relaxations, and it is the combination that matters. A low sottotetto that could not be certified as living space under a 2.70 m rule, and could not be reclassified under the old change-of-use rules, may now clear both tests. On a city flat bought by the square metre, converting 25 sqm of accessory space into counted living space is a material change in what the property is worth.

Two cautions attach. Municipal instruments still govern, and a comune can be stricter than the national floor, so the answer is local. And the reclassification is a procedure with a cost and a professional behind it, not a relabelling exercise: the space has to meet the requirements as certified, not merely resemble a room.

The related provision on removable glazed enclosures belongs to the same logic. Enclosing a balcony or a portico with a removable glazed system was a widespread alteration and a widespread irregularity. Bringing those systems within the works needing no permission regularised a large stock of them at a stroke, subject to the usual carve-out where a constraint applies.

## What it did not do

The reform is narrower than the coverage suggested, and the gaps are where buyers get hurt.

**It is not a condono.** Italy's amnesties in 1985, 1994 and 2003 forgave completed unlawful work on payment of a sum. Salva Casa changes how you assess whether work was unlawful and how minor breaches can be cured. A building with no title remains a building with no title.

**It does not touch the protected-area regime.** Landscape and heritage constraints run on their own authorisation track. A property inside a vincolo still needs the relevant consent, and the simplified routes narrow sharply. Where a purchase involves anything listed or scenic, [heritage restrictions on Italian property](/guides/heritage-restricted-property-italy/) is the governing page, not this one.

**It does not override regions and comuni.** Building regulation in Italy is layered, and local instruments can be more demanding than the national floor. What a decree permits, a comune's own rules may not.

**It does not settle the position retroactively.** The habitability relaxations apply from the date they took effect and are framed as transitional, pending a health ministry decree that will set performance-based requirements. Anyone relying on them should ask the professional whether the rule they are relying on is still the operative one at the date of the certification.

**It does not remove the survey.** Every one of these provisions is applied by a professional making a certification, on measurements somebody has to take. The decree changed the thresholds; it did not change who establishes the facts, which is still a [geometra or equivalent](/guides/geometra-role-italy-property/).

## How it works in practice

None of this is self-executing, and the procedure is short enough to describe.

A professional, normally a geometra, an architect or an engineer, measures the property, pulls the planning file at the comune, and compares the two. Where the divergence falls inside a tolerance band, they certify. Where it does not, they assess whether the simplified route under article 36-bis is available, and if it is, they prepare and file the application.

Timescales are set by the comune rather than nationally, and vary widely. A certification of stato legittimo on an ordinary flat is a matter of weeks once the file has been retrieved; a regularisation application is a matter of months. That difference is the reason to start before the preliminary contract rather than after, since the deposit stage in Italy commits a buyer earlier than most foreign buyers expect.

Costs split in two. The professional fee is the predictable part, in the hundreds of euro for a certification on a normal residential property and higher for a full regularisation file. The payment to the comune, where a regularisation is needed, is assessed case by case by reference to the value the work added, so it cannot be quoted in advance and should be estimated by the professional before you commit.

One habit is worth adopting whatever the property. Ask for the certification of stato legittimo by name, and ask for it to be produced before the compromesso. A seller who has one will hand it over. A seller who does not will have to commission it, which is exactly the work you wanted done.

## What a buyer should do with this

**Where the reform helps**

- A property refused a mortgage on a small dimensional divergence: remeasure against the new band
- A minor breach previously called unregularisable: test it against article 36-bis
- A low-ceilinged or small unit that could not be certified habitable: test it against the 2.40 m and 20 sqm thresholds
- An enclosed balcony that was a standing irregularity: frequently no longer one
- A deal that stalled before 2024 on a technicality: worth reopening

**Where it changes nothing**

- Added volume or an added storey with no title
- Anything inside a landscape or heritage constraint
- An amnesty application from 1985, 1994 or 2003 that was filed and never decided
- Work completed after 24 May 2024, which falls outside the tolerance provision
- A comune whose own rules are stricter than the national floor

The honest summary is that Salva Casa moved a boundary rather than removing one. It made a band of ordinary, decades-old, harmless irregularity certifiable, which is a real improvement for buyers of older Italian property and a poor reason to skip a survey. The full check sequence remains as set out in [due diligence before the compromesso](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/), and the cadastral half of it in [what a visura catastale does not prove](/guides/visura-catastale-italy-explained/).
