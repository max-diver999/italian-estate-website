## Buying property in Abruzzo, where the discount is real and so is the reason for it

**Quick answer:** Abruzzo asks about EUR 1,192 per square metre against a national average nearer EUR 2,188, which makes it one of the cheapest places in central Italy to buy a house with a coastline attached. The discount is genuine. So are its causes: a thin international resale market, a coast that empties in winter, and a seismic classification that covers most of the region and decides what a survey has to look at.

The region is small, mainland, and unusually varied for its size. Thirty minutes separates Adriatic beach towns from the Gran Sasso massif, and the two behave like different markets with different buyers.

## The geography, because most English sources get it wrong

Four provinces: L'Aquila, Teramo, Pescara and Chieti.

**L'Aquila** is the regional capital and sits inland in the mountains. It is not the largest city and it is not on the sea. Its province is the highest and coldest part of Abruzzo, and the 2009 earthquake is still visible in both the built environment and the market.

**Pescara** is the largest city and the commercial centre, on the Adriatic, with the region's airport and its busiest station.

**Chieti** city is inland, roughly fifteen kilometres from the coast, on a ridge above the Pescara valley. Its province, confusingly, holds a long stretch of coastline: Francavilla al Mare, Ortona, and Vasto in the south. When a listing says "Chieti coast" it means the province, not the city.

**Teramo** province holds the northern beaches, Giulianova, Roseto and Alba Adriatica, and carries the region's highest asking prices as a result.

Getting this straight matters commercially. A buyer who thinks they are buying near the sea in Chieti city arrives to find a hill town, and a buyer who books flights to L'Aquila expecting a beach has misread the map.

## What the prices actually say

| Where | Asking, per square metre | What sets it |
|---|---|---|
| Abruzzo, region-wide | about EUR 1,192 | Idealista, first quarter 2026 |
| Italy, for comparison | about EUR 2,188 | National average |
| Pescara city | about EUR 2,140 | Coast, jobs, transport; the regional premium |
| Chieti city | about EUR 1,080 | The cheapest provincial capital in the region |
| Chieti province | about EUR 1,187 | Includes the southern coastal towns |
| Teramo province | EUR 1,623 to 1,645 | Northern beach resorts, the dearest band |
| L'Aquila province | EUR 1,092 to 1,202 | Mountain, seasonal, thin |

Two readings follow. The first is that Pescara is nearly twice the regional average, so anyone quoting "Abruzzo from EUR 1,192" and showing you a Pescara seafront flat is quoting the wrong number. The second is that the gap between Pescara and Chieti city, roughly two to one for fifteen kilometres of distance, is one of the sharpest short-range price gradients in central Italy.

Rents are flatter than prices. Pescara and Chieti both sit near EUR 6.96 to EUR 6.97 per square metre a month, which means the yield gradient runs the opposite way to the price gradient: the cheaper city produces the better gross return.

## The seismic question, which is not optional here

Abruzzo is one of Italy's most seismically classified regions, and the 2009 L'Aquila earthquake is recent enough that its consequences are still working through both the building stock and local practice.

For a buyer this changes the survey rather than the decision. Three things belong in the file before a price is agreed:

**Which seismic zone the comune sits in**, because that determines the code the building was meant to meet and the code any works must meet now.

**When the building was built or last structurally intervened**, since the standards tightened repeatedly and a 1970s block is a different proposition from a 2015 one.

**Whether any reinforcement work has been done, and whether it was permitted.** Post-earthquake reconstruction in this region was extensive and not all of it is cleanly documented.

None of that argues against buying. It argues for instructing a structural engineer rather than only a geometra, and for treating the classification as a pricing input. The national framework is in [seismic zones and Italian property](/guides/seismic-zones-italy-property/), and the compliance side, which frequently overlaps, in [due diligence before the compromesso](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/).

## Which part suits which buyer

**Pescara** suits a buyer who wants the property let all year. Hospital employment, public administration and the Chieti-Pescara university campuses give it a tenant base that does not disappear in October. Furnished twelve-month leases on centro stock return roughly 4.5% to 5.5% gross. It is also the easiest part of the region to sell in, which matters more than it sounds.

**Chieti** is the value position. City asking prices near EUR 1,080 per square metre against rents almost identical to Pescara's produce the best gross yields in the region, and the Università degli Studi "Gabriele d'Annunzio" Chieti-Pescara supplies tenants each intake. The trade is a smaller city, thinner foreign enquiry, and hill-town stock where lifts and parking are often absent from pre-1990 buildings.

**The Teramo and Chieti coasts** suit short letting, with everything that implies. A good season reaches 5% to 6% gross; the winter is genuinely empty rather than quiet, management runs 25% to 35% of gross where you are not there, and the annual figure depends entirely on how the void is budgeted. Short letting also needs the national registration code and, in many comuni, a local notification before marketing begins.

**L'Aquila province** suits lifestyle buyers who want mountains and accept that resale is slow and largely domestic. It is the thinnest market in the region and the one where mispriced peripheral stock sits longest.

## The towns buyers actually end up looking at

Beyond the four provincial capitals, enquiry concentrates in a short list, and each one behaves differently.

**Vasto and Ortona**, on the southern Chieti coast, are working towns with beaches rather than resorts, which keeps prices below the Teramo strip and gives them some winter life. **Francavilla al Mare** sits close enough to Pescara to borrow its rental market.

**Giulianova, Roseto degli Abruzzi and Alba Adriatica** form the northern beach run in Teramo province. These are the most seasonal places in the region and the most expensive per square metre. They are also where short-let competition is heaviest, which matters if a purchase depends on nightly rates.

**Sulmona**, inland in L'Aquila province, is the town most often bought for itself rather than for a return: a walled centre in a mountain basin, cheap by any Italian standard, with almost no international market behind it. **Scanno** and the villages around the national parks are the same proposition with more scenery and less liquidity.

The rule that holds across all of them is that a small Abruzzo town has one buyer pool, and it is domestic. That is fine for a purchase intended to be used and slow for one intended to be sold.

## Getting there, which shapes the letting market

Abruzzo has an airport at Pescara with seasonal and low-cost routes rather than a year-round network, so most foreign arrivals still come through Rome. That drive takes roughly two hours on the A24 or A25, mountain motorways that are genuinely spectacular and occasionally closed by snow in winter.

The Adriatic rail line runs the length of the coast and connects Pescara north to Ancona and Bologna and south toward Bari. Inland, connections thin quickly: L'Aquila is served but slowly, and much of the mountain interior is a car proposition.

For a letting position this matters directly. Coastal stock within walking distance of a station lets to people who arrive without a car, which is a meaningfully larger pool than stock that requires one. Inland, a car is assumed, and the question becomes parking rather than transport.

## Against Marche and Molise

Abruzzo asks roughly 20% below Marche, which averages near EUR 1,499 per square metre, and Ancona at about EUR 1,541 has employment depth no inland Abruzzo town matches. Marche also carries more international recognition, which shows up at exit rather than at purchase.

Against [Molise](/guides/molise-property-investment-guide/) the comparison runs the other way. Molise is cheaper again and considerably thinner, with a smaller population and less transport. Abruzzo sits between the two: cheaper than Marche, more liquid than Molise, and the only one of the three with an airport of its own.

Within the region, the closest comparison most buyers actually make is set out in [Pescara against Chieti](/compare/pescara-vs-chieti-property/).

## What a purchase costs and how it is financed

A non-resident buying a second home should carry **10% to 15%** of the price again in transaction costs. The largest line is registration tax at **9%**, charged on cadastral value rather than on the price agreed, which in a low-value region works considerably in the buyer's favour.

Rental income on a furnished long lease is taxed at **21%** under cedolare secca, with no deductions available against it. The full purchase-cost breakdown sits in [the cost of buying property in Italy](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/) and the yield arithmetic in [the Italian rental yield guide](/guides/italy-rental-yield-guide/).

On financing, Italian banks lend to non-residents at roughly **50% to 60%** of the valuation rather than of the price, and the valuation is the number that decides the deal. In a region where portal asking prices run ahead of closed sales, that gap is where non-resident purchases most often stall.

## Before you commit

| What to establish | Why it decides the price here |
|---|---|
| The seismic zone and the building's structural history | The single largest unpriced risk in this region |
| Building compliance on terraces, extensions and enclosures | A deed cannot be signed where the titles do not match the building |
| Three years of condominium accounts, with the assembly minutes | Reinforcement and lift works appear in the minutes before the bill |
| Whether short letting is permitted at that specific address | Comune rules vary and the registration code is national |
| Closed OMI comparables in the same quartiere | Asking prices lead closed sales, most visibly in spring |

The wider eligibility position for a foreign buyer is in [buying property in Italy as a foreigner](/guides/buy-property-italy-foreigner/). One local habit is worth adopting: in a market this thin, ask how long the property has been listed. A twelve-month listing in Chieti or L'Aquila province is not a bargain waiting to happen, it is a price the market has already answered.

## Advantages and disadvantages

### Advantages

- One of the lowest entry costs in central Italy, at roughly 55% of the national average per square metre
- Coast and high mountains inside one small region, thirty minutes apart
- A year-round tenant base in Pescara and Chieti from hospitals, administration and the university
- Registration tax charged on cadastral value, which favours low-value markets disproportionately
- An airport and direct rail, which neither Molise nor much of inland Marche can claim

### Disadvantages

- Seismic classification across most of the region, with survey costs to match
- A thin international resale market outside Pescara and the northern beaches
- Genuine winter emptiness on the coast rather than a quiet season
- Hill-town stock frequently without lifts or parking, which limits the tenant pool
- Valuations that lag portal asking prices, which complicates non-resident financing
