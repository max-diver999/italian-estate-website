## Retiring to Italy: what the decision actually involves

**Quick answer:** Italy has no visa called a retirement visa. Retirees use the elective residence visa, which requires about EUR 31,160 a year in passive income and **prohibits work of any kind**. The compensation is a 7% flat tax on all foreign income for ten years if you settle in a qualifying southern comune, a ceiling that rose to 30,000 residents in April 2026. Healthcare costs roughly EUR 2,000 a year and has a renewal trap in it.

Four things decide whether this works: the visa, the tax, the healthcare, and where you actually live. They interact, and most guides treat them separately.

## The visa, and the sentence that ends most plans

The elective residence visa is built around one idea: you support yourself from income you do not work for. Retirees with capital rather than pension income sometimes look at the other route instead, and its thresholds are in [the investor visa requirements](/guides/italy-investor-visa-requirements-2026/). Pensions, annuities, rental income from property abroad, dividends and investment returns all count. Salary does not, and neither does freelance income.

**Work of any kind is prohibited.** Not restricted, prohibited. That includes remote work for an employer outside Italy, occasional consulting for former clients, and running a business from a laptop. People arrive believing the prohibition is about taking Italian jobs. It is not. It is about the basis on which you were admitted, and it is checked again at every annual renewal.

If keeping some work is part of the plan, this is the wrong visa and the [digital nomad visa](/guides/italy-digital-nomad-visa/) is the one to read, because it is built for exactly the opposite case.

**The income test** is EUR 31,160 a year for the main applicant, with 20% added for a dependent spouse and 5% for each dependent child, which puts a couple near EUR 38,000. Two things are worth knowing beyond the arithmetic.

Consulates want a margin. Filing at exactly the minimum is the most common way to be refused, because a consulate is judging whether the income is sufficient and durable rather than whether it clears a line.

And the calculation has been contested. In July 2025 the administrative court in Rome overturned a refusal where the consulate had applied EUR 31,000 **per person** rather than the household calculation. If you are told each adult must independently show the full figure, that reading has been challenged successfully.

The mechanics of the application, the document set and the renewal cycle are set out in full in our [elective residence visa guide](/guides/italy-elective-residence-visa-property/).

## The 7% flat tax, and what changed in April 2026

This is the part of retiring to Italy that most changes the arithmetic, and the rules moved this year.

A foreign pensioner who transfers tax residence to a qualifying Italian comune can elect a **flat 7% on all foreign-source income**, for the year of transfer and the nine following. Not just the pension: foreign dividends, foreign interest, rental income from property abroad and capital gains on foreign investments all fall inside it.

The conditions are narrow and specific.

| Condition | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Where you settle | A comune in Abruzzo, Molise, Campania, Puglia, Basilicata, Calabria, Sicily or Sardinia |
| Size of that comune | **No more than 30,000 residents**, raised from 20,000 by Article 26 of Law 11 March 2026 no. 34, in force 7 April 2026 |
| Prior tax residence | Not an Italian tax resident in any of the five preceding years |
| Income covered | All foreign-source income, not only the pension |
| Duration | The year of transfer plus the nine following, ten tax periods in all |

The April 2026 change matters more than it sounds. Raising the ceiling from 20,000 to 30,000 residents added **74 municipalities**, and towns in that band are a different proposition from villages of a few thousand: they tend to have a hospital, a station, a functioning high street and other foreigners. Most guides still print 20,000.

Two cautions. The election is all or nothing across your foreign income, so where a treaty already gives you a better outcome on part of it, 7% can be worse than the default. And the geography is not negotiable: the regime follows the comune you are resident in, so it decides where you live rather than the other way around. If your heart is set on Tuscany or the lakes, this regime is not available to you, and the comparison to make is against the [flat tax regime for new residents](/guides/italy-flat-tax-regime-new-residents/), which is national but built for much larger incomes.

## Healthcare, and the December trap

Residency does not include healthcare. A non-EU retiree with an elective residence permit joins the national health service, the SSN, by **voluntary registration**, and pays for it.

The contribution runs from **EUR 2,000 to about EUR 2,789 a year**, scaled to income, and it is paid through an F24 form. Private cover is the alternative, and many retirees hold both: the SSN for the system, private insurance for speed.

The trap is administrative rather than financial. **The contribution is tied to the calendar year and expires on 31 December regardless of when you paid it.** Register in November and you pay for two months, then pay again in January. Where the timing is yours to choose, joining early in a year rather than late in one is worth a full contribution.

Budget the healthcare line at EUR 2,000 to EUR 2,800 for one person, before any private policy, and remember that the visa itself requires insurance from day one, before SSN registration is even possible.

## What the tax actually looks like if the 7% regime does not apply

Outside the southern regime, a resident retiree is taxed on worldwide income through the ordinary IRPEF scale. The 2026 budget law cut the middle band, so the current figures are:

| Taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to EUR 28,000 | 23% |
| EUR 28,001 to EUR 50,000 | 33% |
| Above EUR 50,000 | 43% |

Regional and municipal surcharges sit on top, typically adding 1% to 3% depending on where you live.

Against those numbers the 7% regime is worth roughly a fifth of a EUR 40,000 pension every year for ten years, which is why the choice of comune deserves more thought than the choice of house.

Whether your pension is taxable in Italy at all depends on the treaty and on the type of pension. Government and military pensions are commonly taxable only in the paying state, while private and occupational pensions frequently become taxable in Italy on residence. United States citizens remain within the US filing system whatever Italy does. None of this can be resolved from a website, and it is the single most valuable thing to take to a commercialista before committing.

## The same pension, two comuni: a worked example

Numbers make the geography argument better than adjectives do. Take a couple with a combined foreign pension and investment income of EUR 60,000 a year, no Italian-source income, moving in the same month.

**Couple A settles in a comune of 12,000 in Puglia** and elects the regime. Their Italian tax on the whole EUR 60,000 is 7%, or EUR 4,200. Add SSN voluntary registration for two, roughly EUR 4,000 to EUR 5,600 depending on how the contribution is assessed.

**Couple B settles near Florence**, where the regime is not available. The same EUR 60,000, split between two taxpayers at EUR 30,000 each, runs through the ordinary bands: 23% on the first EUR 28,000 of each and 33% on the remainder, which is about EUR 13,100 before regional and municipal surcharges of 1% to 3% on top.

The gap is close to **EUR 9,000 a year**, and it runs for ten years. That is not a rounding difference; it is the cost of a decent car every three years, or the entire renovation budget on a modest house.

Three qualifications keep this honest. The regime taxes all foreign income at 7%, so a household whose treaty already exempts a government pension in Italy may be worse off electing it. The comparison ignores what the same EUR 60,000 pays in each place, and Puglia is materially cheaper to live in than Florence, which widens the gap further. And the regime ends after ten years, at which point the ordinary scale applies and the decision to stay is made again on different terms.

The point is not that everyone should retire to the south. It is that the tax question should be answered before the location question, because in this specific case the tax rules dictate the map.

## Where to retire, and why the tax rules answer it

Most retirement guides sort Italian regions by scenery. If the 7% regime is in play, the sorting is done for you: the qualifying comuni are in the south and the islands, at 30,000 residents or fewer.

That is less of a constraint than it reads. Puglia, Abruzzo, Sicily and Sardinia contain a large share of the country's affordable, warm, coastal and hill-town stock, and the towns in the 20,000 to 30,000 band newly brought into the regime tend to be exactly the ones with services rather than the depopulating villages the phrase "small comune" suggests.

If the regime does not apply, the sorting reverts to ordinary criteria: winter population, distance from a hospital, whether the town functions in February, and airport access for family visits. Those are covered in [where to live in Italy](/guides/best-places-to-live-italy/), and the household budget in [what a month in Italy costs](/guides/cost-of-living-italy/).

One point specific to retirement rather than relocation generally. Ageing in a hill town is not the same as holidaying in one. Steep streets, no lift in a historic building, and a twenty-minute drive to the nearest hospital are all charming at 60 and constraining at 80. Buyers who take this seriously look at ground floors, lift access, and driving distance to an ospedale, and they look at it before falling in love with a view.

## Buying or renting, and in what order

Nothing in the visa requires you to own. A registered lease of twelve months or more satisfies the accommodation requirement, and it is what most successful applicants file with, because a lease can be arranged from abroad in weeks while a purchase cannot.

The order that works is rent first, buy second, and it is worth being blunt about why. The comune you choose determines whether the 7% regime applies, and that is a tax decision you should take with an accountant rather than an estate agent. Renting for a first year lets you make it without a property tying you to the answer. It also means you experience the town in winter before committing, which is the single cheapest piece of due diligence available on this whole plan.

Where a purchase is the intention from the start, the transaction costs are the same as for any foreign buyer and are set out in [what buying in Italy costs to complete](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-italy/). The annual carry, principally IMU on a second home, is in [Italian property taxes for foreign buyers](/guides/italy-property-taxes-foreign-buyers-guide/). Neither is affected by which visa you hold.

## The five-year and ten-year marks

Two horizons are worth planning around from the start.

After **five years** of continuous legal residence you may apply for the EU long-term residence permit, which removes the annual renewal cycle and its income proof.

After **ten years** of legal residence a non-EU national may apply for Italian citizenship by naturalisation. For anyone with Italian ancestry, that route is usually slower and harder than the alternative: see [citizenship by descent](/guides/italy-citizenship-by-descent/), where the qualifying rules changed materially in 2025 and are worth checking before assuming either path.

The renewals in between are not a formality. Each one re-tests income, accommodation and insurance, and the work prohibition applies throughout.

## What sends people home

The failures are rarely financial and rarely about the visa.

**The work prohibition,** discovered after arrival by someone who intended to keep consulting. There is no workaround inside this permit; the fix is a different visa or a different plan.

**Bureaucratic fatigue.** The codice fiscale, the questura appointment, the residency registration at the comune, the SSN enrolment, the annual renewal. Each step is manageable and the sequence is relentless, and it is materially harder without functional Italian.

**Isolation in the wrong town.** A comune of 8,000 in February is a different country from the same comune in August. Renting for a winter before buying is the cheapest insurance available, and almost nobody does it.

**Healthcare distance,** which is the one that ends retirements rather than delaying them. Ask where the nearest emergency department is and how long the drive takes at night, before the purchase rather than after.
