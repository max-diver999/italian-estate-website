Quick answer: an Italian offer is asymmetric by design, the proposta d'acquisto binds you from signature and the seller only from acceptance, and the national asking-to-closed gap has run 7-10% for years, so both the paper and the number reward preparation. The winning pattern is unglamorous: a documented price case, 1-2 protective conditions, a 7-15 day validity, and a caparra cheque you are genuinely willing to commit. This guide covers the form's mechanics, discount data by market temperature, the levers that actually move sellers in Genoa or anywhere else, and the handoff into the [compromesso](/guides/compromesso-italy-property-contract/) that acceptance triggers.

## What is a proposta d'acquisto and when does it bind?

A proposta d'acquisto is a signed written offer, usually on the agency's form, that binds the buyer immediately and irrevocably for its validity window of typically 7-15 days, while the seller remains free until acceptance; the seller's signature converts the same sheet into an enforceable preliminary contract, and the attached cheque, commonly 1-5% of price, upgrades from annex to caparra at that instant.

The binding sequence, step by step:

1. **You sign**: irrevocable for the window (art. 1329 c.c.); withdrawal before expiry is itself a breach.
2. **Seller considers**: they may accept, refuse, counter, or let it lapse; your cheque sits uncashed.
3. **Acceptance communicated**: a contract now exists on the proposta's terms; the caparra takes effect.
4. **Registration**: accepted propostas containing the essential terms are registrable like preliminaries.

| Moment | You | Seller |
|---|---|---|
| After your signature | bound for the validity window | free |
| After acceptance | bound (caparra at risk) | bound (double-caparra rule) |
| On your breach | caparra forfeited | may keep it and walk, or sue |
| On seller's breach | claim double the caparra | — |

Asymmetry in the proposta's design is deliberate and old: article 1329 of the civil code makes an offer declared irrevocable binding for its stated period, while the seller's freedom until acceptance preserves their auction option, so the buyer pays with commitment for the privilege of naming a price. The practical consequences all flow downhill from that trade: a buyer who signs at €340,000 on a 15-day window has sold a free 15-day option on their own money, the agent may lawfully present competing offers throughout, and a seller can accept a better number on day 14 leaving the first bidder nothing but a returned cheque. Professionals compress the exposure two ways, shorter windows of 7-10 days and conditions that survive acceptance, and amateurs discover the exposure by living through it.

## How much below asking should you open?

Opening numbers are a data question, not a bravado question: the Bank of Italy quarterly agent survey has tracked average asking-to-closed discounts between 7% and 10% in recent years, down from roughly 15% at the 2013 trough, with city and segment spreads wider than the national line. Your opening belongs 2-5 points below your target close, positioned by market temperature.

| Market state | Signals | Sensible opening |
|---|---|---|
| Hot (Milan centre, Bologna) | under 60 days on market, multiple viewings | 3-5% below asking |
| Balanced (most cities) | 60-120 days, steady interest | 6-10% below |
| Slow (rural, oversupplied coast) | 120+ days, price cuts logged | 10-15% below, documented |
| Distressed signals | probate, transfer, vacant + arrears | value-based, ignore asking |

Building the documented case takes one evening and three sources:

- Closed comparables from the OMI zone tables and portal sold-data, price per square metre against this property's.
- The listing's own history: days on market and prior cuts, both visible on major portals.
- Defect pricing: the [due diligence](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/) findings converted into contractor quotes.

**Insider tip:** ask the agent one question early: «quanto è trattabile?» (how negotiable is it?). Agents answer it more honestly than buyers expect, they earn on closings, not on asking prices, and the answer calibrates your opening better than any national average.

## Which conditions belong in the offer?

Conditions are the only legitimate machinery for reopening an accepted deal, so every protection you may need later must ride on the proposta itself: financing, survey, and conformity clauses are market-standard, cost little seller goodwill when drafted with deadlines, and convert future discoveries from disputes into price adjustments. An unconditional offer is a discount you give away silently.

The three standard clauses, in the wording logic that makes them bite:

1. **Mortgage condition**: offer void and caparra returned if a named bank declines a stated amount by a stated date; sellers accept 30-45 day windows routinely.
2. **Survey / defect condition**: completion subject to a geometra's inspection; defects above a euro threshold reopen price or void the deal.
3. **Conformity condition**: cadastral plans and permits must match reality at the seller's cost before compromesso; this clause alone defuses the most common Italian closing killer.

| Clause | Cost in seller goodwill | Value when it fires |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage (dated, named bank) | moderate; cash beats you | full caparra back |
| Survey with threshold | low | €5,000-30,000 repricing leverage |
| Conformity at seller's cost | low; reads as professional | months of delay avoided |

Condition drafting quality is measurable in euros when clauses fire: a mortgage clause naming «financing» generically invites dispute over whether declining one bank exhausts it, while one naming the bank, the amount (€280,000), and the deadline (45 days) returns the caparra mechanically on a dated refusal letter. Survey clauses work the same way: «subject to satisfactory inspection» is litigation bait, «defects whose remediation exceeds €10,000 by written quote entitle the buyer to withdraw or renegotiate» is a price tool. Italian courts enforce conditions as written and construe ambiguity against the interest asserting it, so the 30 minutes a competent lawyer spends sharpening three short sentences is the cheapest insurance in the whole transaction, priced at €200-500 against a caparra of €10,000-20,000 already at stake on acceptance.

Price and conditions trade against each other explicitly in Italian practice: a clean cash offer 2-3% lower routinely beats a higher conditional one, because the seller's risk-adjusted arithmetic discounts conditions at their failure probability. Decide which currency you are paying in, price or certainty, before the form is on the table, with financing groundwork from the [non-resident mortgage guide](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-italy/) done first if certainty is your bid.

## How do you set the caparra and validity window?

The cheque attached to a proposta typically runs €5,000-20,000 or 1-5% of price at the offer stage, deliberately smaller than the 10-20% caparra the compromesso will later carry, and the validity window standard is 7-15 days: long enough for a considered acceptance, short enough that your capital and irrevocability are not parked as a free option for the agent to shop.

Calibration rules that practitioners actually use:

- **Cheque size** signals seriousness more than it secures the deal at this stage; €10,000 on a €350,000 Genoa apartment reads committed without overexposing you before due diligence.
- **Window length** tracks decision complexity: 7 days for an individual seller, 10-15 where heirs or a company board must convene.
- **Named payee and uncashed status**: the cheque is made to the seller, held by the agent, banked only on acceptance; wiring money at the offer stage, sometimes requested, forfeits this containment and should be refused politely.
- **Caparra type**: confirmatoria (the default, with the forfeit/double rule) unless you specifically negotiate penitenziale, which prices a clean exit for both sides; the [compromesso guide](/guides/compromesso-italy-property-contract/) maps the difference in depth.

Withdrawal mathematics at this stage stay small by design, which is exactly why the offer stage, not the compromesso, is where aggressive price experiments belong: a lapsed proposta costs nothing but time, while a compromesso breach costs the full 10-20% caparra, a 10-40x difference in the price of changing your mind.

## What levers move Italian sellers, and which backfire?

Every lever that works is arithmetic, not theatre: documented comparables, defect quotes, completion dates matched to the seller's own next purchase, and visible proof of funds all reprice the deal in the seller's head, while a bare lowball with nothing attached triggers the national pastime of waiting buyers out. Practitioners put 60% of negotiation value in non-price terms.

The lever board, ranked by observed effect:

| Lever | Why it works | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| Proof of funds / pre-approval letter | converts your number into a certainty | 2-4% price equivalent |
| Flexible or fast completion | solves the seller's chain problem | 1-3% |
| Defects priced in writing | makes the discount their surveyor's fault, not your greed | quote value, often €10,000+ |
| Closed comparables attached | moves the anchor legitimately | 2-5% off inflated askings |
| As-is acceptance of minor items | removes friction | goodwill that closes gaps |

The backfire list is shorter and absolute: insulting the property to its owner (Italian sellers sell homes, not assets), serial re-offers after refusals without new information, fake competing-purchase pressure, and any suggestion of under-declared price, which is both illegal and, since cadastral-value taxation makes honest declaration cheap for buyers, pointless, as the [cadastral vs market price guide](/guides/cadastral-value-vs-market-price-italy/) explains. Cross-cultural patience deserves a number too: Italian sale negotiations average 2-4 rounds over 2-3 weeks, and buyers who treat a counter at round two as failure are misreading a market where the counter is the invitation.

Distinct buyer scenarios reward different offer architectures, and matching the template to your profile is worth more than any single clause. The cash-funded holiday-home buyer maximises leverage by pairing a 7-day validity with zero financing conditions and a conformity clause only: certainty is their currency, and it buys 2-4% off. The mortgage-dependent buyer inverts the design, a named-bank clause with a 45-day deadline and a slightly higher number, paying in price for the protection. The investor bidding on tenanted stock adds a third pattern: an offer conditioned on verified rent rolls and registered lease terms, since yield claims in listings survive checking about as often as asking prices survive negotiation.

Sellers read these architectures fluently even when buyers assemble them by accident, which is the strongest argument for assembling them on purpose. An offer whose conditions, validity and price tell one coherent story, a serious counterpart who knows what they need and what it costs, produces counters within days; an offer whose parts contradict each other produces silence, and silence in Italian negotiation is not suspense but triage.

## What happens between acceptance and the compromesso?

Acceptance starts a 2-4 week administrative interlude with real legal weight: the accepted proposta is already a binding preliminary contract, the agent's commission has legally matured, registration duties arise (0.5% on the caparra where registered), and both sides' lawyers turn the one-page offer into the full compromesso with its schedules, deadlines and the 10-20% caparra step-up.

The buyer's checklist for the interlude, in order:

1. **Register or verify registration** of the accepted proposta where it contains all essential terms; unregistered acceptance is valid between the parties but invisible to the world.
2. **Launch full due diligence** now, not at compromesso: visure, permits, condominium minutes; discoveries feed the conditions while they can still bite.
3. **Fix the money calendar**: caparra step-up amount, [transfer timing](/guides/transferring-money-to-italy-property/), and FX hedging if funding is non-euro.
4. **Agree the compromesso date in writing** with a longstop; drift here is where deals quietly die.
5. **Confirm commission terms**: Italian agents' right matures at the accepted proposta, so the invoice arriving before the compromesso is normal, not sharp practice.

Registration mechanics in the interlude confuse buyers annually, so the rules deserve plain statement: an accepted proposta containing the essential terms, parties, property, price, is already a preliminary contract, registrable with the Agenzia delle Entrate within 30 days at fixed duty of €200 plus 0.5% on any caparra, and the later formal compromesso then registers without doubling the proportional charges already paid. Skipping registration saves that outlay but costs opposability: unregistered, your position exists between the parties and nowhere else. Transcription at the conservatoria, a separate, notarised step, adds protection against the seller's creditors and double-sales for buyers with long gaps to closing. The cost ladder runs from €200 for basic registration to €1,500-2,500 for a notarised, transcribed preliminare, and where the gap to rogito exceeds 6 months, the top rung is rarely the wrong buy.

The step-up from offer-stage cheque to full caparra is the moment your risk profile changes by an order of magnitude, and it deserves the same seriousness as the rogito itself: from here, the path and its remaining hazards run through the [compromesso guide](/guides/compromesso-italy-property-contract/) and onward through the closing sequence in the [step-by-step guide](/guides/how-to-buy-italy-property-step-by-step/), with the classic failure patterns catalogued among the [mistakes foreign buyers make](/guides/mistakes-foreign-buyers-italy/).
