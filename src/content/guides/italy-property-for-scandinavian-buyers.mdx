**Quick answer:** there is no Scandinavian answer to this question. A Danish resident pays an annual Danish property tax on an Italian house whether or not it earns anything. A Norwegian resident adds it to a wealth tax base at a discounted valuation. A Swedish resident pays nothing recurring at home at all. Treating the three as one market, which is how this page was previously written, gives the wrong answer to two readers out of three.

<TldrBlock text="Denmark: ejendomsværdiskat on the foreign house, annually, Danish rates · Norway: enters formuesskatt at roughly 30-40% of market value, above the threshold · Sweden: no wealth tax since 2007, no fee on foreign property · rental income converges, all three tax with credit"/>

Buyers from the three Scandinavian countries look alike from an Italian estate agent's desk. They buy in similar places, at similar tickets, for similar reasons. What happens afterwards in their own tax systems is not similar at all, and the difference is annual and permanent rather than a one-off at purchase.

## What does each country do with the asset?

The divergence is about how each state treats owning property, and the three have made genuinely different choices.

Denmark applies ejendomsværdiskat, a property value tax, to residents on the property they own, and the charge follows the owner rather than the border. A Danish resident with a holiday home abroad pays the Danish property value tax on it in the same way and at the same rates as on a Danish home. It is not a tax on income and does not wait for the house to earn anything.

Norway does not have a property value tax of that kind but does have formuesskatt, a net wealth tax on residents assessed on worldwide assets. A holiday home abroad enters that base. The valuation is not market value: holiday property is brought in at a fraction, commonly in the range of 30% to 40%, and the wealth tax applies to net wealth above the personal threshold, which sits in the region of NOK 1.7 million per person.

Sweden has neither. The wealth tax was abolished in 2007 and the inheritance tax in 2005, and the municipal property fee that Swedish homeowners pay is charged on property situated in Sweden. A Swedish resident's Italian house attracts no recurring Swedish charge.

| | Denmark | Norway | Sweden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual charge on the foreign house | Yes, property value tax | Yes, via wealth tax | No |
| Basis | Danish rates on the property | 30% to 40% of market value | Not applicable |
| Threshold before it bites | None specific to the asset | Personal wealth threshold | Not applicable |
| Charged when the house is empty | Yes | Yes | No |

## Why does this change which property makes sense?

Because two of the three charges are indifferent to whether the house earns anything, and the third does not exist.

A Danish buyer purchasing a holiday home for family use is taking on two annual charges on an asset with no income: Italian IMU at second-home rates, covered in our [IMU guide](/guides/imu-property-tax-italy/), and the Danish property value tax. Neither is enormous individually and together they change the calculation on a property that will be used for six weeks a year and stand empty for the rest.

A Norwegian buyer in the same position faces a charge that scales with their overall wealth position rather than with the house, and the valuation discount softens it substantially. For a household below the wealth threshold the Italian purchase can add nothing at all; for one already above it, the addition is real but computed on €150,000 to €200,000 of a €500,000 house rather than on the full value.

A Swedish buyer faces only the Italian side.

The practical consequence is that the same property produces three different total costs of ownership, and a letting strategy is worth more to a Danish owner than to a Swedish one because it gives the annual charges something to be paid out of.

- Model the home-country annual charge alongside IMU rather than separately
- Where the property will stand empty most of the year, the Danish position is the one most improved by letting
- **Insider tip:** for a Norwegian household near the wealth threshold, the purchase's effect depends on the rest of the balance sheet, so model it at household level rather than per property

## Where do the three converge?

On income, and it is worth stating clearly because the divergence above can give the impression that nothing is shared.

All three countries tax their residents on worldwide income, including Italian rent, and all three relieve double taxation by credit under the relevant convention with Italy. Italy taxes first, at 21% on the first residential unit let short-term in a year and 26% on the second and beyond under the flat regime, or under ordinary IRPEF with expenses. Our [cedolare secca guide](/guides/cedolare-secca-italy-rental-property/) works the Italian comparison.

The home-country charge then applies to the same income with a credit for the Italian tax. Because Italian rental tax is substantial, the credit frequently absorbs most or all of the home charge, so the practical outcome on the income side is similar for a Dane, a Norwegian and a Swede.

They also converge on Italian purchase mechanics. Denmark and Sweden are EU member states and Norway is in the European Economic Area, so none of them meets the reciprocity condition and none faces a stay limit at the property. The [purchase sequence](/guides/how-to-buy-italy-property-step-by-step/) is identical for all three.

## What does the valuation question look like from the north?

Both the Danish and the Norwegian charges need a value for an Italian house, and neither country has a mechanism for producing one.

Denmark assesses foreign property for its property value tax on a basis derived from the property's value, and the Norwegian wealth tax needs a market value before applying its discount. In both cases the owner supplies the figure and has to be able to support it.

Italy offers two numbers and neither is the answer. The cadastral value is a fiscal construct used for registration tax and IMU and sits far below market, as our [cadastral value guide](/guides/cadastral-value-vs-market-price-italy/) explains. The purchase price is good evidence in year one and decays afterwards.

- Keep a consistent valuation basis year to year, since inconsistency invites questions in both systems
- A written valuation from an Italian professional is the strongest support and is inexpensive relative to the tax
- Documented renovation spending raises the value and should be recorded, since it also raises the cost base on an eventual sale

## Do they diverge on inheritance as well?

They do, along the same three-way split and with the same countries in the same positions, which is unusual enough to be worth relying on.

Sweden abolished inheritance and gift tax in 2005, so an Italian house passing to Swedish heirs attracts no Swedish charge. Norway abolished its arveavgift in 2014, reaching the same outcome by a later route. Denmark retained its estate duty, boafgift, which applies at 15% above a threshold for close family and at a substantially higher combined rate for more distant beneficiaries.

Italy charges its own succession tax on Italian property whoever inherits, and for a spouse or child the rate is 4% above a franchigia of €1 million per beneficiary, which means most family holiday homes produce no Italian charge at all.

The combination is therefore clean for two of the three and not for the Danish family, who face a Danish charge with no Italian tax to credit against it, because Italy took nothing.

| On death, Italian house to a child | Italy | Home country |
|---|---|---|
| Swedish family | Usually nothing under the franchigia | Nothing since 2005 |
| Norwegian family | Usually nothing under the franchigia | Nothing since 2014 |
| Danish family | Usually nothing under the franchigia | Boafgift at 15% above the threshold |

- Danish families should treat the Italian house as a Danish estate asset from the day of purchase
- The Italian succession declaration is due within 12 months of death regardless of which country charges tax
- The EU Succession Regulation lets a national choose their own law to govern the estate, and Norway is outside that regime, which is a further asymmetry for Norwegian owners

## What about the currency each of them is buying with?

The same three-way split appears a third time, and it is the one buyers notice soonest because it shows up on the day the money moves.

The Danish krone is held in a narrow band against the euro under the European exchange rate mechanism, so a Danish buyer converting to euro is doing something close to a fixed-rate exchange. The purchase price agreed in March is very nearly the purchase price paid in September, and an Italian mortgage is a liability in a currency their own is tied to.

The Norwegian krone and the Swedish krona both float freely against the euro, and both have moved substantially within single years. A Norwegian or Swedish buyer who agrees a price in euro and completes 4 months later is carrying a real and unhedged exposure over that interval, and one that recurs on every subsequent transfer for works, furniture or annual charges.

That produces a genuine difference in how the three should approach an Italian mortgage. For a Danish buyer, borrowing in euro against a euro asset is close to currency-neutral. For a Norwegian or Swedish buyer it is a currency position taken alongside a property position, and the two need to be judged together rather than separately.

- **Risk:** a Norwegian or Swedish buyer treating the euro price as fixed between compromesso and rogito
- Fix the euro cost early where the timetable allows rather than converting on the deed date
- Staged payments on an off-plan purchase spread the exposure rather than concentrating it

## Which buyer scenarios does this separate?

**The Danish family buying a summer house in Puglia.** Two annual charges, no income, and a strong argument for either letting part of the season or accepting the running cost consciously. Our [Puglia guide](/guides/puglia-property-investment-guide/) covers the market this profile most often buys in.

**The Norwegian household below the wealth threshold.** Effectively the same position as a Swedish buyer, with the charge appearing only if the household's overall wealth rises past the line later. Worth revisiting rather than assuming permanence.

**The Norwegian household above it.** A real annual cost, computed on a discounted valuation, and one that scales if the Italian market rises. The decision framework is a household balance-sheet question rather than a property question.

**The Swedish buyer.** The simplest position among the three and among the cleanest in this entire cluster: Italian tax only, no recurring Swedish charge on the asset, and a credit mechanism on any rental income.

**The buyer who moves between the three.** More common in this region than elsewhere, and it changes the answer entirely. A Swede who relocates to Copenhagen acquires a Danish annual charge on a house they already owned, with no transaction and no notice.

For the Italian rates every one of these owners pays, the [prima casa comparison](/guides/italy-prima-casa-vs-second-home-tax/) explains the second-home position, and the [selling guide](/guides/selling-property-italy-foreigner/) deals with the disposal itself. Finnish and Icelandic buyers should not read across from any of the three positions above, since their own systems answer the ownership question differently again.
