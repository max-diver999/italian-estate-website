**Quick answer:** A large share of the housing demand around Como comes from people who earn francs across the frontier and spend euro at home. The tax basis of that arrangement changed on 17 July 2023, and whether it applies to a given household depends on when they started and on which comune they live in.

<TldrBlock text="Agreement of 23 December 2020, ratified by L. 83/2023, in force 17 July 2023 · new cross-border workers taxed in both states, Swiss withholding at 80% of ordinary rates plus Italian tax with relief · those who worked in Switzerland between 31 December 2018 and 17 July 2023 stay taxed only in Switzerland until retirement · the status requires residence in a comune within the 20 km border strip · the Italian exempt band on that income is €10,000" />

Property writing about Como is about villas. The market that actually determines prices in most of the province is about commuting, and the rules that govern it were rewritten three years ago. For a buyer looking at ordinary housing anywhere near the frontier, that change is more consequential than anything happening on the lake.

## What changed and when

The agreement between Italy and Switzerland on the taxation of cross-border workers was signed on 23 December 2020, ratified by Italy through law 83 of 2023, and entered into force on 17 July 2023.

Its central feature is a division of the population into two groups by date.

| Group | Definition | Tax position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New cross-border workers | Acquired the status after 17 July 2023 | Taxed in both states |
| Existing cross-border workers | Worked as employees in Switzerland between 31 December 2018 and 17 July 2023 | Taxed only in Switzerland, until retirement |

For the new group, tax is withheld at source in the canton of work at rates equal to 80% of the ordinary ones, and Italian income tax is then charged in Italy with relief for the Swiss tax paid. For the existing group, the previous arrangement continues.

That produces the situation that defines the local labour market: two people doing the same job for the same employer, living on the same street, on different net incomes because one started before a date and one after.

## Why this is a property question

Because eligibility depends on where you live. The status attaches to residence in a comune whose territory falls, wholly or partly, within the border strip running to 20 kilometres from the frontier. A household whose income depends on that status cannot simply buy the house they prefer; they have to buy inside the line.

The consequences for the local market are visible in prices and are frequently misread by outside buyers.

- Comuni inside the strip carry a demand premium that local Italian salaries alone would not support
- The premium is not uniform: it reflects commuting time to specific Swiss workplaces as well as the strip boundary
- A property just outside the strip serves a different and smaller buyer pool, whatever its qualities
- Rental demand in these comuni is driven by the same population, which makes it more stable than tourist demand and more exposed to cross-border policy

For a foreign investor, the practical instruction is to establish, for any specific comune, whether it falls within the strip, and to treat that as a hard input rather than an approximation. The information is definite and obtainable.

## What the Italian side provides

The Italian implementing provisions include an exempt band on cross-border employment income of €10,000, together with treatment of family allowances paid by the social security institution of the state where the work is performed.

Those provisions reduce the impact of concurrent taxation without eliminating it. For any individual household the net effect depends on the canton, the gross salary, the family situation and which of the two groups they fall into, which is why a competent local accountant is worth more than any general statement in an article.

What can be said generally is that the direction is settled. Households acquiring the status now face a higher combined burden than households that acquired it before July 2023, and that gap is a permanent feature of the market rather than a transition.

## What a franc income does to a purchase

Earning in one currency and buying in another introduces a set of practical issues that Italian lenders handle in their own ways rather than uniformly.

1. Affordability assessment has to account for exchange rate movement, and lenders apply their own haircuts
2. Documentation of foreign employment and foreign payslips takes longer to assemble and to verify
3. Some lenders are considerably more comfortable with this profile than others, largely because of where they operate
4. The mortgage will be in euro while the income is in francs, which leaves the borrower carrying the currency risk
5. Anti-money-laundering documentation on funds arriving from a non-EU state is more detailed

None of this prevents borrowing and all of it is smoother when raised at the start. A buyer who discovers at the valuation stage that their lender is uncomfortable with franc income has lost weeks.

For Swiss nationals buying in Italy as investors rather than commuters, the position is a different one again and is set out in the [guide for Swiss buyers](/guides/italy-property-for-swiss-buyers/).

## What the change did to the local market

Border property markets are shaped by net income rather than gross, and a change to the tax basis of that income works through prices with a lag.

The immediate effect was on the marginal buyer. A household considering a move to a border comune after July 2023 faces a different net income from an equivalent household that moved in 2019, and that changes what they can borrow and what they will pay. Since existing workers keep the old treatment until retirement, the two groups coexist and will do for decades, which slows the adjustment rather than preventing it.

The second effect is on the composition of demand. A larger share of the local pool is now people whose combined tax position depends on Italian rates, which makes them more sensitive to Italian property taxation, to running costs and to the second-home charge. Owners letting to this group should expect tenants to pay closer attention to charges than they used to.

The third effect is on the boundary itself. When the tax status attaches to residence in a defined strip, the strip becomes a value line rather than an administrative one. Buyers should therefore be sceptical of comparisons between two properties that appear equivalent but sit on opposite sides of it.

- Establish which group a prospective tenant belongs to, since it determines their disposable income
- Expect the demand premium inside the strip to persist while the two groups coexist
- Treat any advice written before July 2023 as describing a regime that no longer applies to new arrivals

## A model for a border comune flat

Take a flat inside the strip: 79 square metres with parking, bought at €228,000 all in, let unfurnished to a commuting household at €790 a month.

| Line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Annual rent | €9,480 |
| Condominium charges borne by the owner | €710 |
| Vacancy allowance, 3 weeks a year | €545 |
| Gross yield on the all-in price | 4.2% |

The figures are illustrative. What the model is meant to show is that this is a plain residential letting business with an unusual demand driver behind it: not tourism, not students, but employment across an international frontier whose tax treatment is set by treaty and can change again. An owner underwriting it should therefore be tracking a bilateral agreement rather than a local rental index, which is an unusual thing to ask of a residential landlord and is the price of the demand premium.

## Two markets that share a lake

The province contains two property markets that have very little to do with each other, and most confusion here comes from treating them as one.

The **frontier market** is ordinary housing in border comuni, bought by households with Swiss employment, priced on commuting time and on the tax status described above. It is deep, transactional and responsive to policy.

The **lake market** is second homes and villas, bought by people whose income comes from elsewhere entirely, priced on setting, frontage and scarcity. It is thin, slow and responsive to international sentiment rather than to Italian or Swiss tax rules.

- A comparison of yields between the two is close to meaningless, because the assets serve different purposes
- The frontier market's risk is policy; the lake market's risk is liquidity
- Only the frontier market has a demand base that lives here year round
- [Bellagio](/areas/bellagio/) sits firmly in the second market and carries its own specific issues around lakeside rights

For buyers weighing the lake against other Italian options, the [Garda and Como comparison](/compare/lake-garda-vs-lake-como-property/), the [Tuscany and Como comparison](/compare/tuscany-vs-lake-como-property/) and the [Liguria and Como comparison](/compare/lake-como-vs-liguria-property/) each set out a different version of that choice. For urban alternatives within reach, [Milan's Navigli](/areas/milan-navigli/) is the obvious contrast.

## Where the demand actually sits

Commuting geography, not scenery, decides which comuni carry the premium, and it is finer grained than a 20 km radius suggests.

The crossing points are the bottleneck. A comune close to a busy crossing with a queue at seven in the morning can be a worse commute than one further away on a quieter route, and residents know exactly which is which. The rail link matters too, since a household with a station within walking distance avoids the crossing queue entirely and can run one car instead of two.

The Swiss workplace matters as well. Employment concentrates in specific cantonal centres, and a comune well placed for one is not necessarily well placed for another. A buyer letting to this market should know which workplaces their address serves.

- Test the actual commute at the actual hour, not the mapping application's estimate
- Prefer addresses with a rail option, which insulates a tenant from road conditions
- Ask a local agent which employers the street's residents work for
- Remember that the strip boundary is a legal line and the commute is a practical one, and both matter

## What happens on resale

The buyer pool for a house inside the strip is largely the same population that occupies it: households with cross-border employment. That is a real pool and it is not a large one, and it is sensitive to the same policy the current owner is.

Two implications follow for anyone underwriting an exit. A change in the cross-border regime affects the value and the liquidity of the asset simultaneously, since it removes buyers rather than merely reducing what they will pay. And the property's appeal to a buyer without cross-border employment is materially lower, because they are paying a premium for a status they cannot use.

## Diligence points specific to the province

Assemble the file described in the [due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/), then answer three questions that only a frontier purchase raises.

**Confirm the comune's position relative to the border strip**, in writing, if the purchase depends on it.

**Establish commuting reality rather than map distance.** Crossing points have queues, and a comune 12 kilometres from the frontier by road can be 45 min from a workplace at seven in the morning. Local buyers price this precisely and remote buyers do not.

**Check the building's heating and energy performance.** Winter here is real, the housing stock includes a great deal of poorly insulated post-war construction, and running costs are a live part of what a commuting household compares.

Modelling rental returns in the frontier comuni is a different exercise from modelling them on the lake, and the [rental yield guide](/guides/italy-rental-yield-guide/) sets out the method for both. The input that matters most here is that tenant demand comes from employment across a border, and that the terms of that employment were changed by statute in 2023.
