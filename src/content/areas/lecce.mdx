**Quick answer:** Every parcel in the province of Lecce sits inside a demarcated zone under European plant health law. The duties that come with that, felling, monitoring and controlled replanting, attach to the land and pass to whoever owns it. For anyone buying a country property here, that is a title question rather than an agricultural one.

The Salento sold to foreign buyers for two decades was a landscape: ancient olive trees, dry-stone walls, a masseria in the middle of them. A bacterium first identified in the area in 2013 changed what that landscape contains, and changed what a purchase in it involves. The legal consequences are more precise, and more manageable, than the alarming photographs suggest, but they have to be understood before an offer rather than after.

## What the regime is, in law

Plant health here is European law applied through regional administration. The relevant instrument is Commission implementing regulation (EU) 2020/1201 of 14 August 2020 on measures to prevent the introduction and spread of Xylella fastidiosa within the Union, and its Annex III Part A lists the Salento infected zone as comprising the entire province of Lecce, the province of Brindisi and a number of municipalities in the province of Taranto.

The architecture of the regime has three levels, and which level applies to a given parcel decides what its owner must do.

| Level | Definition | Consequence for the owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infected area | A radius of at least 50 metres around an infected plant | Removal duties and controls |
| Buffer zone under eradication | 2.5 kilometres | Intensive monitoring, removals |
| Buffer zone under containment | 5 kilometres | Monitoring, vector control |
| Wider infected zone | Listed area, including all of Lecce province | Movement and planting restrictions |

The felling requirement is the part that changed most since the emergency years. The area subject to mandatory cutting around an infected plant was reduced from 100 metres to 50 metres, which materially narrows the exposure of a holding where a single infection is found, and reflects the regime's shift from emergency eradication toward long-term management.

## Why this is a property question rather than a farming one

Because the obligations attach to the land. A purchaser who acquires a holding in a demarcated area acquires whatever duties apply to it, and the fact that the trees were planted by someone else three centuries ago is not a defence.

That produces a specific and avoidable failure. A buyer views a masseria with an olive grove, prices it as a property with a working grove, completes, and then discovers that the grove is a liability requiring clearance under supervision, with replanting constrained by species and by the zone's status. The gap between those two valuations is not marginal.

The disciplined sequence is short:

1. Identify every land parcel by its registry identifier, since the phytosanitary records key on parcels rather than on addresses
2. Obtain the current demarcation status for each parcel from the regional phytosanitary service
3. Ask whether any order has been served on the current owner, and whether it has been complied with
4. Establish the condition of the trees by inspection, distinguishing dead standing trees from living ones
5. Price the clearance and the replanting as works, using the [renovation and works cost guide](/guides/italy-property-renovation-costs-guide/) as a starting point and adding for supervision

Step three is the one buyers skip. An outstanding compliance order is a known, documented liability, and it does not disappear at the deed.

## What can be planted afterwards?

Replanting is permitted and it is regulated. In areas where eradication measures are applied, planting of citrus, peach, apricot and plum is authorised on the basis that they are resistant to the relevant subspecies of the bacterium. For olives, the route runs through cultivars identified as tolerant rather than through whatever the previous grove contained, which is why replanted groves in the Salento look different from the ones they replaced.

Two consequences follow for a buyer's plan.

The landscape a replanted holding produces is a young one. Olive trees planted now are not the trees in the photographs, and they will not be within the holding period of any normal buyer. A purchase justified by the appearance of an ancient grove should be justified by something else.

The agricultural economics change too. A grove of tolerant cultivars planted at modern spacing is a different farming proposition from a grove of monumental trees, in yield, in mechanisation and in cost. That is not worse, and in production terms it is frequently better, but it is not what was there.

## The trees themselves may be protected

A second regime sits over the first and buyers frequently discover it only when they try to clear a grove. Regional law 14 of 4 June 2007, on the protection and enhancement of the landscape of Puglia's monumental olive trees, prohibits the damaging, felling, uprooting and trade of monumental olive trees, including isolated ones, on the grounds of their productive, ecological, hydrogeological, historical and landscape functions.

The mechanism is a regional list. Trees and groves entered on it are automatically subject to landscape constraint as diffuse landscape assets, and municipalities must identify them in their planning instruments. The list is maintained and updated by regional resolution, so its content changes over time and has to be checked against the parcel rather than assumed. Authorisation for otherwise prohibited activity is possible in defined circumstances, including reasons of public utility, which is a route rather than an exemption.

Two regimes therefore meet on the same land. Plant health law can require removal; landscape law can prohibit it. Reconciling the two is done through the competent authorities rather than by the owner deciding which rule to follow, and the practical answer for a purchaser is procedural:

- Establish whether any tree or grove on the parcel appears on the regional monumental list
- Establish the parcel's demarcation status under the plant health regime
- Where both apply, require evidence of how the seller has been instructed to reconcile them
- Treat any clearance plan not backed by an authorisation as an assumption rather than a plan

| Regime | Instrument | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plant health | Reg. (EU) 2020/1201 | Removal, monitoring, replanting control |
| Landscape | L.R. Puglia 14/2007 | Prohibits felling and uprooting of listed trees |
| Planning | Municipal instrument | Must identify listed groves |

## Does any of this touch the city?

Directly, no. An apartment in the baroque centre of Lecce carries no phytosanitary obligation, and its diligence questions are the ordinary Italian ones: the correspondence between the cadastral plan and the building, the condominium's position, the heritage constraints on a facade in a protected historic centre. The [due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-italy-property/) covers that file.

Indirectly, the connection is real and worth stating honestly. A significant share of the visitor economy that supports letting demand in the city came for the countryside as much as for the architecture, and the countryside changed. That is a demand-side consideration rather than a legal one, and it cuts both ways: the city's own attractions are undamaged, and the province has been investing in replanting and in the landscape's recovery.

For buyers whose interest is the town rather than the land, the practical implication is simply to underwrite the city on the city's own merits: the baroque centre, the university, the regional administration and the summer season, rather than on an image of the surrounding groves.

## What the clearance actually involves

Removing a dead olive grove is a works project with a sequence, and the sequence has authorisations in it.

The first step is establishing which trees may be removed and on what authority, given that plant health duties and landscape protection can both apply. The second is the physical work: felling, extraction of stumps where required, and disposal of material under whatever restrictions apply to movement of plant material from a demarcated area. The third is site preparation for whatever replaces the grove, which on land that has carried olives for centuries is not a trivial agronomic exercise.

Costs scale with the size of the trees rather than with the hectare count, which is why a holding of monumental trees is more expensive to clear than a modern grove of the same area. Access matters as much as it does for a building project, since machinery has to reach the land.

- Confirm the authorisation position before quoting the work, not after
- Ask how material will be disposed of and whether movement restrictions apply
- Price stump removal separately, since it is frequently excluded from a felling quotation
- Establish whether any replanting obligation or incentive applies to the parcel

## What does a well-structured purchase look like here?

Four elements, and none of them is exotic.

**Separate the buildings from the land in the analysis, even if they are sold together.** The masseria has a construction value, a permit history and a renovation cost. The land has a phytosanitary status, a demarcation position and a replanting path. Combining them into one price per hectare hides both.

**Make the phytosanitary file a condition.** Production of the demarcation status and of any orders should be a term of the offer, in the same way that production of the condominium accounts is for an apartment.

**Budget the clearance.** Removing dead olive trees at scale is a real cost with equipment and disposal in it, and it is frequently underestimated by buyers who have priced a renovation but not a clearance.

**Check what the planning instrument permits before assuming a conversion.** Turning agricultural buildings into accommodation is a planning question in Puglia as everywhere, and rural building conversions in a protected landscape are narrower than buyers expect. The [Puglia guide](/guides/puglia-property-investment-guide/) covers the regional picture, and the neighbouring markets of the [Valle d'Itria](/areas/valle-d-itria/), [Ostuni](/areas/ostuni/) and [Carovigno](/areas/carovigno/) sit under related but not identical constraints.

## What the city itself offers a buyer

Set the countryside aside and Lecce is a substantial provincial capital with an unusually complete baroque centre, a university, regional administration and a summer season that is genuinely busy. Its apartment stock is conventional, its prices are low against comparable Italian historic centres, and its constraints are the familiar ones: heritage supervision on the exterior, the frequent mismatch between the cadastral plan and the building, and condominium arrangements in buildings that were single houses before they were divided.

The local building stone, a soft limestone that carves easily, is the reason the city looks the way it does and it is also a maintenance consideration. It weathers, it absorbs moisture, and restoration of a carved facade is specialist work under supervision. A buyer should ask what the building's facade has cost over the past decade before assuming it will cost nothing over the next one.

## How does this compare with the rest of the south?

The regulated status is what distinguishes this province, and the comparison worth making is not with Tuscany but with the other southern options. The [Sicily and Puglia comparison](/compare/sicily-vs-puglia-property/) sets out how the two regions differ on cost, access and demand, and [Polignano a Mare](/areas/polignano-a-mare/) illustrates the coastal alternative within the same region.

The honest summary of Lecce is that the province is cheaper than it was for a reason that is documented rather than rumoured, that the reason is a set of obligations a buyer can read and price, and that the buyers who lose money here are the ones who bought a photograph. The buyers who do well are the ones who priced a clearance project, obtained the file and planned on a young landscape.
